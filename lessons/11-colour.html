Lesson 11

Colour

Lesson 10 gave rules of thumb for colour. This lesson gives the machinery: a colour space in which the numbers mean what you see, palettes built by rule instead of picked by eye, and checks that tell you when a combination will fail.

Why HSL lets you down

HSL is convenient because hue, saturation and lightness sound like the way we talk about colour. Its flaw is that its "lightness" is not the lightness you perceive. Yellow at 50% lightness is glaringly bright. Blue at 50% is dark. So a rule such as "keep lightness fixed and rotate the hue" produces an uneven, blotchy set of colours, and a rule such as "light text on a dark colour" works for some hues and fails for others.

OKLCH, published by Björn Ottosson in 2020 and now part of CSS, fixes this. It has the same three ideas, but measured so that equal numbers look equal:

L, lightness
0 is black, 1 is white. Two colours with the same L look equally light, whatever their hue.
C, chroma
How colourful: 0 is grey. The maximum depends on hue and lightness, and is about 0.3 for the most vivid colours a screen can show.
H, hue
An angle in degrees. Roughly: 30 red, 90 yellow, 145 green, 250 blue, 320 magenta.

PA.oklch(L, C, H) returns a colour for drawing, and PA.oklchRGB(L, C, H) returns its red, green and blue values. The example draws the hue circle twice at one fixed lightness, then shows each strip as a camera with black-and-white film would see it. The OKLCH strip is an even grey. The HSL strip is not.

Palettes by rule

With a trustworthy colour space, a palette becomes a small program. Decide the roles first, then compute a colour for each:

RoleRule
BackgroundVery light or very dark, almost no chroma, tinted slightly toward the base hue
DominantThe base hue at middling lightness and moderate chroma
SupportA neighbouring hue, or the dominant at another lightness
AccentA hue far from the dominant, at high chroma, used rarely
InkThe opposite end of lightness from the background, low chroma

Harmony is geometry on the hue wheel

The classical colour harmonies are simply patterns of angles:

SchemeHue angles from the baseCharacter
Monochrome0Calm, unified. Variety from lightness only.
Analogous−30, 0, +30Harmonious, natural
Complementary0, 180Maximum contrast, energetic
Split complementary0, 150, 210Contrast with less tension
Triadic0, 120, 240Balanced, playful

The example builds a five-role palette from one base hue and a scheme, and immediately tries it on a small composition, with the roles used in the proportions of Lesson 10: mostly dominant, some support, a little accent. Judging a palette as swatches is misleading. Judge it in use.

Contrast: will it be readable?

Whether two colours can be told apart, and whether text is legible on a background, depends almost entirely on the difference in their lightness. The standard measure is the contrast ratio used in web accessibility guidelines, which runs from 1 (identical) to 21 (black on white). The usual thresholds are 4.5 for body text and 3 for large text and for shapes that must be distinguished.

PA.contrast(rgbA, rgbB) computes it. In a generator this is a test you run on every palette, so that a seed can never produce pale yellow text on white. If a pair fails, move one colour's lightness until it passes.

Notice that the yellow and green inks need a much lower lightness than the blue before they pass. Because OKLCH lightness is perceptual, the repair can work on that one number and leave hue and chroma alone.

Gradients that stay clean

A gradient between two colours is a lerp, but the result depends on which numbers you interpolate. Blending red, green and blue values directly drags the middle of the gradient through a duller, darker colour: blue to yellow passes through grey. Blending lightness, chroma and hue keeps the middle as vivid as the ends.

Hue needs one extra care. It is an angle, so there are two ways round from one hue to another. Take the shorter one, unless you deliberately want a rainbow.

More colour tools

ToolUse
Lightness rampsOne hue at five to nine lightness steps: the basis of every design system's colour scale

Chroma falloffReduce chroma toward the edges or the background to push things back in space
Temperature contrastWarm accents on a cool field, or the reverse
SamplingTake palettes from photographs or paintings you admire, then express them as roles
Colour-blind checksAbout one man in twelve confuses red and green. Never let hue alone carry meaning: back it with lightness.

Key ideas

  • OKLCH lightness matches what you see, so rules about lightness actually hold.
  • Build palettes from roles: background, dominant, support, accent, ink.
  • Colour harmonies are fixed patterns of hue angles.
  • Contrast is a lightness difference. Compute the ratio and repair failures automatically.
  • Blend in lightness, chroma and hue for clean gradients, and take the short way round the hue circle.
  • Judge a palette in a composition, never as swatches.

Common mistakes

  • High chroma everywhere. Vivid colours only read as vivid beside quiet ones.
  • Asking for a colour the screen cannot show, such as very light and very saturated blue. It is silently clipped, which shifts the hue. Lower the chroma.
  • Choosing colours on a bright monitor in a dark room. Check on a phone and in daylight.

Challenge 11

The Palette Machine

Build a generator that turns a seed into a complete, usable palette, and proves it by applying the palette to three different compositions.

Requirements

  • A function makePalette(seed) that returns named roles: at least background, dominant, support, accent and ink.
  • Colours are computed in OKLCH, with the scheme (the pattern of hue angles) chosen from the seed by weighted chance.
  • Both light and dark palettes can come out.
  • The generator guarantees a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 between ink and background, and at least 3 between the dominant colour and the background, by repairing lightness.
  • A display showing, for one seed: the swatches with their roles and contrast figures, and the palette applied to three test compositions of different kinds.
  • A contact sheet of at least 20 seeds.

Hints

  • Reuse compositions from your earlier challenges as the test pieces. Give each a palette argument.
  • Restrict the ranges. Chroma between 0.08 and 0.2 and a small set of lightness values give far better results than full freedom.
  • When a seed gives an ugly palette, find the rule that allowed it and tighten that rule.

Stretch goals

  • Export the palette as CSS custom properties and as a JSON file.
  • Add a lightness ramp of seven steps for the dominant hue.
  • Simulate red-green colour blindness and check that roles stay distinguishable.

You are done when

  • All 20 palettes on the contact sheet are usable, and no two look alike.
  • Every palette passes its contrast checks without your looking.
