Lesson 16

Growth Algorithms

Coral, frost, veins, lichens and river deltas are not designed. They accumulate, a small piece at a time, each piece placed by a local rule. This lesson builds three algorithms that grow forms the same way, and all three are short.

Growing versus drawing

Lesson 6 made branching forms by recursion: the whole tree was decided from the top down, in one pass. Growth algorithms work the other way. They start from a seed and add to it step by step, and each addition depends on what is already there and on what is around it. The form is a record of its own history.

That gives grown forms two qualities that recursive ones lack. They respond to their surroundings: they avoid obstacles, fill the space available, and compete with themselves. And they are unrepeatable in detail, though unmistakable in kind.

All three algorithms here follow the pattern of Lesson 8: a state, a local rule, and a step repeated many times.

Diffusion-limited aggregation

Release a particle far away and let it wander at random. When it touches the growing cluster, it sticks there for good. Release another. That is the whole of diffusion-limited aggregation, described by Witten and Sander in 1981 to explain how soot and metal deposits form.

The result is a branching, feathery shape, and the reason is worth understanding. A wandering particle is far more likely to meet the tip of a branch than to find its way down into a gap between branches. So tips grow, gaps stay empty, and any small bump becomes a branch. The same instability shapes frost on a window, lightning, and mineral dendrites in rock.

Variations change the character completely. Let particles stick with a probability below 1 and they penetrate deeper, giving denser, mossier growth. Start from a line along the bottom instead of a point and you get a forest of ferns. Give the walkers a slight drift in one direction and the cluster leans into the wind.

Space colonisation

Trees do not branch at random. They grow toward light and unoccupied space. Space colonisation, published by Adam Runions and colleagues in 2007, models exactly that, and produces the most natural-looking branching of any simple algorithm.

  1. Scatter attraction points through the volume the plant should fill: the shape of its crown.
  2. Each attraction point pulls on the nearest branch node, if one is within reach.
  3. Every node that is being pulled grows a new node a short step in the average direction of its pulls.
  4. Any attraction point that a branch has come very close to is removed. That space is now colonised.
  5. Repeat until no attraction points are left within reach.

Branches split wherever two groups of points pull a node in different directions, and they stop where there is nothing left to reach for. The crown shape is entirely decided by where you scatter the points, which makes this a very controllable way to grow a tree, a leaf's veins, a root system or a river network.

Try it: change the crown test to a ring, a heart or a letter (Lesson 12's inside test works here). Lower KILL for denser twigs. For a finished tree, make each branch's thickness depend on how many twigs it carries: count descendants from the tips back to the root.

Differential growth

The third algorithm grows a line, not a branching structure. Take a closed loop of points and apply three rules to every point:

Repulsion
Push away from every other point that is too close.
Cohesion
Stay close to your two neighbours along the line, so that the line stays smooth.
Growth
Keep inserting new points into the line, and split any segment that has become too long.

The line keeps getting longer, but it cannot cross itself, and it has only so much room. So it buckles and folds, and the folds fold again. The result looks like brain coral, the edge of a lettuce leaf, or the surface of a walnut. It is called differential growth because different parts of the form grow at different rates.

Each point here is compared with every other, which limits the example to about a thousand points. With the spatial hashing of Lesson 19 the same algorithm handles tens of thousands, which is where the forms become really intricate.

Guiding growth

What makes growth algorithms useful for design, not just pleasant to watch, is that every one has a place where you can steer it.

ControlHowEffect
BoundaryStop or deflect growth outside a shapeGrown forms that fill a letter, a silhouette, a vessel
FieldLet noise or an image scale the growth rate or the step sizeDense in some regions, sparse in others
Starting stateSeveral seeds, a line, a ringCompeting colonies, forests, frames
ObstaclesRegions that nothing may enterForms that flow round things
Stopping timeHalt early or lateA sprout or a thicket: the same code
RenderingColour by age, thicken by flow, draw only the tipsVery different images from one structure

Other growth systems worth looking up: cellular automata started from a single seed (Lesson 8), reaction–diffusion, Eden growth (the simplest of all: fill a random cell beside the cluster), and physically based models of cracks, erosion and crystal formation.

Key ideas

  • Growth adds to a form step by step, each step depending on what is there already.
  • Diffusion-limited aggregation: random walkers stick on contact. Tips win, so it branches.
  • Space colonisation: attraction points pull branches toward unclaimed space, then vanish.
  • Differential growth: a line that lengthens but may not cross itself must fold.
  • The shape of the available space is your main control.
  • When to stop is an artistic decision.

Common mistakes

  • Letting a growth loop run without a limit on points or steps, until the browser stalls.
  • Releasing diffusion walkers far from the cluster, so that almost all of the time is spent wandering in empty space.
  • Drawing only the final state. Colouring by age or order tells the story of how the form grew.

Challenge 16

A Grown Form

Grow something inside a shape of your own choosing, and present it as a finished image: a specimen, an ornament, a map of somewhere that grew.

Requirements

  • One of the three algorithms, or a combination, written by you and not copied from the examples.
  • Growth is confined to a shape you defined with an inside test: a letter, a leaf, a ring, a silhouette.
  • A field (noise or distance) changes how the form grows in different places.
  • The rendering shows history: age, order or thickness by flow, not a flat colour.
  • A clear stopping rule, and the growth can be watched as it happens.
  • Seeded, with a key for a new seed and one to save a PNG.
  • It reaches at least 3,000 elements without dropping below a comfortable frame rate.

Hints

  • Get the bare algorithm working in an empty square before adding the shape.
  • For space colonisation, scatter the attraction points only where your inside test is true. The form then fills the shape by itself.
  • If it slows down, the cost is in comparing everything with everything. Lesson 19's grid of buckets is the cure.

Stretch goals

  • Two colonies in different colours competing for the same space.
  • Thickness by flow: branches that carry more twigs are drawn wider, as in a real tree or river.
  • Export the result as SVG lines for a plotter.

You are done when

  • The outline of your shape can be recognised from the growth alone.
  • It looks as though it could be something found, not something drawn.
