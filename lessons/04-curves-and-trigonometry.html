Lesson 4

Curves and Trigonometry

Sine and cosine are the two functions that turn "going round" into x and y. With them you can draw circles, waves, spirals, flowers and the looping figures of a spirograph, each from a line or two of maths.

The only trigonometry you need

Picture a point travelling round a circle of radius 1. At angle a, its horizontal position is cos(a) and its vertical position is sin(a). Both swing smoothly between −1 and 1, and both repeat every full turn. That is all sine and cosine are.

From this comes the conversion you will write hundreds of times, from polar coordinates (an angle and a distance) to canvas coordinates:

x = centreX + Math.cos(angle) * radius;
y = centreY + Math.sin(angle) * radius;

Angles are in radians. A full turn is PA.TAU (2π, about 6.283), so a fraction t of a turn is t * PA.TAU. Because the canvas y axis points down, increasing angles go clockwise on screen.

Sine as an oscillator

Used on its own, sine is a smooth back-and-forth motion with three controls:

value = Math.sin(t * frequency + phase) * amplitude
Amplitude
How far it swings.
Frequency
How fast it repeats.
Phase
Where in the cycle it starts. Changing phase over time makes a wave travel.

Give x and y their own oscillators and the point traces a Lissajous figure. Whole-number frequency ratios such as 3:4 give closed knots. This is a parametric curve: both coordinates are functions of a single parameter.

Polar curves: radius as a function of angle

Go round the full circle, but let the radius change as you go. Whatever rule you choose for the radius becomes the outline. A cosine with a whole-number frequency gives petals, a radius that grows steadily gives a spiral, and a small ripple gives a cog.

Try it: change the 4 in the rose to 5, then to 2.5 (and give it 2 turns). Odd, even and fractional values each behave differently, and working out why is a good exercise.

Circles rolling in circles

A spirograph toy rolls a small wheel inside a large ring, with a pen in a hole of the wheel. The pen's path is the sum of two circular motions: the wheel's centre going round the ring, and the pen going round the wheel's centre the other way. Sums of rotations are a deep idea: with enough of them you can draw any closed curve at all.

The golden angle

Sunflower seeds, pine cones and daisies arrange themselves by one rule: each new element is placed 137.5° round from the last and a little further out. That angle, the golden angle, is the one that never lines up with itself, so the elements pack evenly with no gaps and no rows. The arrangement is called phyllotaxis.

Try it: replace golden with 137.3 * Math.PI / 180. A fifth of a degree is enough to break the even packing into visible spiral arms.

Decay: the harmonograph

A harmonograph is a Victorian drawing machine in which swinging pendulums move a pen. It is a Lissajous figure whose amplitude slowly dies away, so the curve spirals inward instead of retracing itself. Multiplying by Math.exp(-damping * t) produces that fading. Frequencies that are very slightly off a whole-number ratio make the figure rotate as it shrinks.

Straight lines that make curves

Curves can also emerge from straight lines. Put n points round a circle and join point i to point i × m. For m = 2 the lines outline a heart-shaped cardioid, for m = 3 a two-lobed curve, and so on. Letting m drift slowly shows the whole family.

Key ideas

  • cos gives x and sin gives y for a point on a circle. Angle and radius convert to a position with one line each.
  • An oscillator has amplitude, frequency and phase.
  • A parametric curve computes x and y from one parameter. A polar curve computes radius from angle.
  • Whole-number frequency ratios close up. Nearly whole ratios rotate slowly. Irrational ones never repeat.
  • The golden angle spreads points evenly round a centre.
  • Multiplying by a decaying exponential turns a repeating figure into a spiral.

Common mistakes

  • Too few steps along a curve, so it shows corners. Use more samples than seems necessary.
  • Stopping a closed curve before it has completed all its turns. Work out the number of turns, as the spirograph example does with gcd.
  • Forgetting that y points down, which mirrors every figure you copy from a maths book.

Challenge 4

The Specimen Sheet

Invent a family of curves and present nine members of it as a 3 × 3 sheet, like a page from a naturalist's notebook. Each specimen comes from its own seed, and each is labelled with the parameters that produced it.

Requirements

  • One function, drawSpecimen(cx, cy, size, seed), draws any member of the family.
  • The family has at least four parameters chosen from the seed, and combines at least two ideas from this lesson (for example a polar rose whose radius is also modulated by a second sine, or a harmonograph with two pendulums per axis).
  • Every curve either closes exactly or fades out gracefully. No loose ends.
  • Colour or line weight changes along the curve. Draw it as many short segments so each can have its own style.
  • Under each specimen, small text lists its seed and parameter values.

Hints

  • Restrict parameters to values that look good. Picking a frequency from [2, 3, 4, 5] usually beats picking any number between 2 and 5.
  • If some seeds give ugly results, narrow the parameter ranges until none do. That narrowing is the real design work.
  • ctx.fillText draws labels; set ctx.font first.

Stretch goals

  • Animate each specimen being drawn from start to finish.
  • Morph smoothly between two parameter sets using PA.lerp on every parameter.

You are done when

  • All nine specimens clearly belong to one family, and no two are alike.
  • You can reproduce any single specimen at full size from the label alone.
