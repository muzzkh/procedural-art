Lesson 3

Noise

Random numbers jump. Clouds, hills, wood grain and water do not: nearby points resemble each other. Noise is randomness with that neighbourly quality, and it is the most used tool in procedural art.

Random versus noise

Call a random function twice and the two results have nothing to do with each other. A noise function is different in two ways:

  • It takes a position as input. Ask for the same position twice and you get the same value.
  • It is smooth. Positions close together give values close together.

Ken Perlin invented the first widely used noise function in 1983, after working on the film Tron, to give computer graphics a less machine-like look. It is still everywhere.

The number 0.01 is the frequency (also called scale). It decides how far you travel through the noise for each pixel. Change it to 0.002 for long gentle swells, or 0.05 for a nervous scribble. Choosing the frequency is the first decision in every noise-based piece.

How noise works

The simplest kind, value noise, takes three steps:

  1. Imagine a grid of points at whole-number coordinates. Give each a fixed random value, using a hash of its coordinates so that the same point always gets the same value.
  2. For a position between grid points, find the four surrounding points.
  3. Blend their four values according to how close you are to each, using a smooth curve so there are no creases at the grid lines.

Try it: replace smooth(x - ix) with plain x - ix and look for the creases along the grid. Then remove the blend entirely by returning hash(ix, iy) to see the raw grid of values.

Perlin noise improves on this by storing a random direction at each grid point instead of a random value, which hides the grid much better. Simplex noise, Perlin's later design, uses a triangular grid and is cheaper in higher dimensions. In use they all behave the same way: a position goes in, a smooth value comes out. PA.noise(seed) gives you Perlin noise as N.get(x, y, z), returning 0 to 1.

Octaves: adding detail

One layer of noise is smooth but dull, like low rolling hills. Real terrain has hills, and on the hills boulders, and on the boulders pebbles. You get that by adding several layers, called octaves, each with a higher frequency and a smaller strength than the last. The result is called fractal Brownian motion, fBm.

Octaves
How many layers. More layers, more fine detail. Four to six is typical.
Lacunarity
How much the frequency grows per layer. Usually 2.
Gain
How much the strength shrinks per layer. Usually 0.5. Higher is rougher.

A noise value becomes a picture when you decide what it means. Here it means height, and height bands become colours.

Noise values crowd around the middle. Although the range is 0 to 1, most values land between about 0.3 and 0.7, and adding octaves narrows that further. That is why the thresholds above are bunched near 0.5. When a noise-driven picture looks washed out, stretch the values with PA.map before using them.

Noise as displacement

Noise does not have to become colour. Feed it into position and it bends geometry. Here a stack of straight horizontal lines is pushed upward by noise, most strongly near the centre. Each line is filled with the background colour before it is stroked, so lines in front hide those behind.

Notice the second noise input, j * 0.25. Each line samples a slightly different row of the same noise, so neighbouring lines are similar but not identical. Using an index as a noise coordinate is a standard way to get a family of related shapes.

Noise in time, and closed loops

Add a third coordinate and move through it over time: the 2D pattern evolves smoothly. This is how you animate anything organically.

The example also shows a second trick. To wrap noise around a closed shape with no visible seam, sample it along a circle in noise space. The end of the circle meets its beginning, so the values match.

Try it: change 1.2, the radius of the circle in noise space. A small radius gives a gently lopsided circle. A large one gives a spiky star.

Domain warping

The most striking noise technique is to use noise to distort the input of more noise. Compute two noise values, use them to shift the position, then sample noise at the shifted position. The result folds and swirls like marble or smoke. The idea was popularised by Inigo Quilez.

Key ideas

  • Noise is a function of position: repeatable, and smooth between neighbours.
  • Frequency sets the size of the features. It is the first thing to tune.
  • Octaves add detail at smaller scales (fBm).
  • Noise is raw material. The art is in what you map it onto.
  • An extra coordinate gives you animation or a family of related variations.
  • Warping the input of noise with more noise gives fluid, marbled structure.

Common mistakes

  • Sampling at whole pixel coordinates without a frequency. N.get(x, y) with pixel values looks like static; you need N.get(x * 0.01, y * 0.01).
  • Expecting values to reach 0 and 1. They cluster round 0.5, so rescale.
  • Using the same noise for two things, such as size and colour, which makes them move in lockstep. Offset one of them (x + 100) or use a second seed.

Challenge 3

Imaginary Topography

Draw a map of a place that does not exist. A seed goes in, and a believable, good-looking terrain map comes out, with coastlines, elevation colours and contour lines.

Requirements

  • Height comes from seeded fBm noise, with a frequency and octave count you have tuned by eye.
  • At least five elevation bands with a palette you chose, including water.
  • Contour lines at regular height intervals.
  • A second, independent noise that changes something visible: moisture that turns grass to desert, a warp of the coastline, or forest cover.
  • Changing the seed gives a different but equally convincing map.

Hints

  • Work per pixel with createImageData, as in example 3.3. A 1000 × 1000 map is a million pixels, which is fine.
  • A point is on a contour when the height is very close to a multiple of the interval: test whether PA.fract(h * 25) is below about 0.06.
  • To make an island, multiply the height by a factor that falls from 1 at the centre to 0 at the edges before applying the colour bands.

Stretch goals

  • Hill shading: compare each pixel's height with the pixel up and to the left, and lighten or darken accordingly.
  • Animate the sea level rising and falling.
  • Draw the contours as vector lines instead of pixels.

You are done when

  • Someone glancing at it reads it as a map without being told.
  • Five different seeds give five maps you would be happy to show.
