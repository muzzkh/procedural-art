Lesson 10

Systems and Finishing

You now have the techniques. What separates a technical demo from a piece of art is everything else: colour, composition, texture, and the discipline of designing a system whose every output is worth keeping.

From technique to artwork

Every example so far demonstrated one algorithm. Finished work rarely does that. It combines two or three techniques, restrains them with a palette and a layout, and adds surface texture so that the image feels made instead of calculated. This lesson covers those finishing skills and then the craft of building a generative system: one program that produces a whole collection.

Colour

Colour is where most beginner work falls down, and the fix is restraint.

  • Use a small fixed palette. A background and three to five inks. Do not pick random hues from the whole wheel.
  • Use weights. One dominant colour, one or two supporting, one rare accent. Roughly 60, 30 and 10 per cent is the designer's rule of thumb. R.weighted exists for this.
  • Contrast in lightness matters more than hue. If your image is unreadable in greyscale, no choice of hue will rescue it.
  • Avoid pure black and pure white. An off-white paper tone and a deep navy or brown-black look far less digital.
  • Vary within a colour. Shifting each use of an ink slightly in lightness makes flat fills feel alive.

When you need a continuous range instead of a fixed set, use a cosine palette (Lesson 9), or blend between two palette colours with PA.mix.

Composition

  • Margins. A clean border round the work makes almost anything look intentional.
  • Hierarchy of scale. A few large elements, some medium, many small. Sizes all alike read as wallpaper.
  • Density contrast. Crowded areas need empty areas beside them. Negative space is part of the picture.
  • A focal point. Give the eye somewhere to land: the largest shape, the single accent colour, the one break in a pattern.
  • Structure first, variation second. Decide the big arrangement, then let randomness work within it.

Try it: make the weights equal, then make all three scales the same size, then remove the grain loop. Each change takes away one reason the image looks designed.

Texture and imperfection

Computers draw perfectly straight lines and perfectly flat fills, and the eye reads that perfection as cold. Much of the finishing craft is adding controlled imperfection:

  • Wobbly lines. Displace points along a line with low-frequency noise.
  • Repeated strokes. Draw each line two or three times with slight differences, at low opacity, as a pencil would.
  • Hatching. Shade with parallel lines instead of flat colour, with density standing in for darkness.
  • Grain and paper. Specks, as above, or a faint noise layer over everything.
  • Blend modes. ctx.globalCompositeOperation = 'multiply' makes layers behave like ink. 'screen' and 'lighter' behave like light.

Designing a system: seeds and traits

Long-form generative art is the practice of releasing an algorithm instead of an image. Collectors or viewers each receive whatever the program produces from a seed that the artist never saw in advance. The form was popularised by platforms such as Art Blocks from 2020 onward. It imposes a hard discipline: you cannot pick favourites. Every output must be good.

The standard structure is to have the seed first choose a small set of named traits, and then have the traits drive the drawing:

seed  →  traits (palette, structure, density, rare features…)  →  render

Traits are chosen by weighted chance, so some are common and some rare. Getting the balance right is the art: enough variety that the collection is interesting, enough consistency that it is recognisably one body of work.

Testing a system: the contact sheet

You cannot judge a generative system from one output. Render many at once, small, side by side, and look for:

  • Failures. Any output that is empty, muddy, broken or ugly. Fix the rule or narrow the parameter range until none remain.
  • Sameness. If you cannot tell outputs apart at a glance, widen the variety.
  • Incoherence. If outputs look like they come from different artists, tighten it.

The contact sheet also demonstrates resolution independence. The artwork function below draws in a unit square, with all coordinates and line widths between 0 and 1. A scale transform maps that square to any size. Sixteen thumbnails or one poster-size print come from the same code and the same seed.

Getting work out of the browser

PNG, high resolution
Create a large canvas (for example 4000 × 4000 pixels, about 33 cm square at 300 dpi), render at that size, and save with canvas.toBlob. This only works if your sketch is resolution independent.
SVG
A vector format: shapes described as text, sharp at any size. Needed for pen plotters, laser cutters and vinyl cutters, and good for print.
Video or GIF
For animated work, save frames one by one and assemble them, or record the canvas with canvas.captureStream() and a MediaRecorder.

SVG is easier than it sounds, because an SVG path is just a string. Build your geometry as path strings; the canvas can preview them through Path2D, and the same strings go into the file.

A working process

  1. Start from a question, not a technique. "What if hatching followed a flow field?" leads somewhere. "I will use Voronoi" does not.
  2. Get something on screen in ten minutes, however crude.
  3. Explore. Change one thing at a time. Save every output that surprises you, along with its seed and settings.
  4. Notice the happy accidents. Bugs are a major source of ideas in this medium. Before you fix one, look at what it drew.
  5. Constrain. Fix the palette, the margin, the ranges. Remove options that do not earn their place.
  6. Test in bulk with a contact sheet and eliminate failures.
  7. Finish. Add texture, check it at full size, export.

Key ideas

  • Limit the palette and weight it. Check contrast in lightness.
  • Compose with margins, a hierarchy of scale, contrast in density, and a focal point.
  • Add controlled imperfection: wobble, repeated strokes, hatching, grain.
  • Structure a system as seed, then traits, then render.
  • Judge a system by many outputs at once, and remove every failure.
  • Draw in unit coordinates so the same seed renders at any size.

Challenge 10 · Capstone

Your Long-Form Collection

Design and build a complete generative system: one program that turns any whole-number seed into a finished artwork in a style that is recognisably yours. This is the piece that goes in a portfolio.

Requirements

  • One seed controls everything. The same seed always produces the identical image.
  • At least two techniques from different lessons, combined so that one affects the other. Examples: circles packed along flow-field streamlines, L-system plants growing on noise terrain, a subdivision layout whose cells each contain a different curve, a reaction–diffusion pattern used as a mask for hatching.
  • At least four named traits chosen by weighted chance, including one rare trait that appears in fewer than one output in ten. Log the traits to the console.
  • Resolution independent. A single SIZE constant can be changed from 1000 to 4000 and the composition is identical, only sharper.
  • A contact-sheet mode showing 25 different seeds in a 5 × 5 grid.
  • Finishing: a margin, a weighted palette, and at least one texture technique.
  • Keys: one for a new seed, one to toggle the contact sheet, one to save a PNG named after the seed.
  • A title for the collection, and three or four sentences in a comment at the top saying what the system is about.

Hints

  • Write render(ctx, seed) so that it draws into a unit square and knows nothing about the canvas size. Single view and contact sheet then differ only in the transform applied before calling it.
  • Build the simplest version of the whole pipeline first: seed, one trait, a plain render, contact sheet. Then improve the drawing. A working contact sheet early on will guide every later decision.
  • Use separate random generators for traits and for drawing, such as PA.rng(seed + '-traits'), so that adding a trait later does not change every existing output.
  • If one trait combination looks bad, either fix it or make the system unable to produce it.

Stretch goals

  • SVG export for a pen plotter.
  • An animated variant in which each piece loops.
  • Read the seed from the page address (location.hash), so every output has a link you can share.

You are done when

  • You generate 25 outputs and would happily frame any one of them.
  • Someone shown three outputs could pick a fourth out of a line-up of other people's work.
  • You can name the trait combination of an output by looking at it.
