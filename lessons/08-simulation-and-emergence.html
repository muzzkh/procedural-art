Lesson 8

Simulation and Emergence

Give many small parts a simple rule about their neighbours, run it thousands of times, and structure appears that no rule mentions: stripes, spots, flocks, veins. That is emergence, and it is the closest procedural art comes to growing a picture.

The shape of every simulation

All the systems in this lesson share one skeleton:

  1. State. A grid of values, or a list of agents.
  2. A local rule. Each cell or agent looks only at its immediate surroundings and decides its next state.
  3. Step. Apply the rule to everything, then repeat.

One technical point matters throughout. When updating a grid, every cell must read the old state of its neighbours. So you keep two grids: read from one, write into the other, then swap. This is called double buffering. Forgetting it is the most common simulation bug.

Cellular automata

The simplest simulation is a row of cells, each on or off. A cell's next state depends on itself and its two neighbours. Those three cells have eight possible patterns, and a rule says, for each of the eight, whether the result is on or off. Eight yes-or-no answers fit in one number from 0 to 255, which is why these are called Rule 30, Rule 90, and so on. Draw each generation below the last and the history becomes a picture.

Rule 90 draws a perfect Sierpinski triangle. Rule 30 produces a stream so unpredictable it has been used as a random number generator. Rule 110 can, in principle, compute anything a computer can. All three are the same eight-entry lookup table.

Life

John Conway's Game of Life (1970) moves the idea to two dimensions. Each cell counts its eight neighbours:

  • A live cell with two or three live neighbours survives.
  • A dead cell with exactly three live neighbours becomes alive.
  • Every other cell is dead in the next generation.

The example also shows a visual technique that applies to every simulation: do not just draw the current state. Here dead cells fade slowly instead of vanishing, so the picture shows history as well as the present.

Reaction–diffusion

In 1952 Alan Turing proposed that animal markings could arise from two chemicals spreading through skin at different speeds while reacting with each other. The Gray–Scott model is the version artists use. Each grid cell holds amounts of two chemicals, A and B:

  • Diffusion: both spread to neighbouring cells, A faster than B.
  • Reaction: one A plus two B becomes three B. B converts A into more of itself.
  • Feed: A is added everywhere at a slow, steady rate.
  • Kill: B is removed at a steady rate.

Two numbers, the feed and kill rates, select the pattern: spots, stripes, mazes, dividing cells, spirals. Small changes give completely different behaviour, so exploring this pair of numbers is the whole game.

Flocking

Simulations can also be made of free-moving agents. Craig Reynolds's boids (1987) reproduce the motion of bird flocks and fish schools with three steering rules, each using only nearby flockmates:

Separation
Steer away from neighbours that are too close.
Alignment
Turn toward the average heading of neighbours.
Cohesion
Move toward the average position of neighbours.

There is no leader and no plan. The flock is a consequence of the three rules.

Agents that write on a grid

The richest systems combine both kinds of state. The slime mould Physarum builds efficient transport networks with no brain. Jeff Jones's 2010 model of it has agents that leave a chemical trail on a grid and are attracted to the trail left by others:

  1. Sense. Each agent samples the trail ahead, ahead-left and ahead-right.
  2. Turn toward whichever is strongest.
  3. Move forward and deposit trail.
  4. The trail grid then diffuses (blurs) and decays (fades).

Agents reinforce paths that other agents use, and unused paths fade. The feedback loop produces branching vein-like networks. One limit keeps the network from collapsing: each cell can hold only so much trail, so a crowded vein is no more attractive than a modest one.

Key ideas

  • State, local rule, step. Complex global patterns come from simple local rules repeated.
  • Read from one buffer and write to another, then swap.
  • Simulations are sensitive. Small parameter changes can give entirely different behaviour, so explore methodically and write down the settings you like.
  • How you draw the state matters as much as the rules: trails, age and colour mapping turn a simulation into an image.
  • For a still image, the moment you stop is a design decision.

Common mistakes

  • Updating a grid in place, so cells read neighbours that have already changed this step.
  • Indexing outside the grid at the edges. Either wrap with % or skip the border cells.
  • Comparing every agent with every other when there are thousands. Use a grid of buckets (Lesson 7).
  • Judging a simulation after ten steps. Many need hundreds before their character shows.

Challenge 8

The Living Wallpaper

Design a two-dimensional cellular automaton of your own and turn it into a continuously moving image that you could leave running on a wall.

Requirements

  • A grid of at least 100 × 100 cells with three or more states per cell, double buffered, with edges that wrap round.
  • A rule you designed or adapted, not plain Game of Life.
  • It must stay interesting for at least 1,000 generations: it must not die out, freeze, or dissolve into static.
  • The colouring shows more than the current state. Use cell age, time since last change, or fading trails.
  • Some rule parameter varies across the canvas or over time, so different regions behave differently.
  • Keys to pause, to step once, and to restart from a new seed.

Hints

  • A good starting point is the cyclic automaton: states are numbered 0 to N−1, and a cell in state k advances to k + 1 (wrapping round) if at least a threshold number of its neighbours are already in state k + 1. Try N between 8 and 16 and thresholds between 1 and 3.
  • Other families worth trying: "Brian's Brain" (on, dying, off), forest fire (tree, burning, empty, with random lightning and regrowth), or Life-like rules with different birth and survival counts.
  • Use a small canvas, such as 200 × 200, and enlarge it with CSS and image-rendering: pixelated.

Stretch goals

  • Let the mouse paint a state into the grid while it runs.
  • Use a larger neighbourhood (radius 2 or 3) and see how the scale of the patterns changes.
  • Build the same idea as a reaction–diffusion system in which feed and kill vary across the canvas.

You are done when

  • You have watched it for two minutes and it was still doing something new.
  • You can describe your rule in two sentences to someone who has not seen the code.
