3D Printing 1

How Printing Works

Before generating models or toolpaths you need a clear picture of what the machine physically does, because every design rule follows from it. This lesson covers the process, the Bambu Lab P1S in particular, and the three places where a procedural designer can intervene.

The process in one paragraph

A filament printer melts a plastic thread and squeezes it through a small nozzle, drawing a thin bead of plastic along a path. It draws one flat layer, moves up a fraction of a millimetre, and draws the next on top. An object is nothing but stacked drawings made of one continuous line. This is called FDM or FFF.

That description already explains the main limits. Each layer needs something underneath to rest on, so steep overhangs sag. The bead has a fixed width, so nothing thinner than it can exist. Layers bond less strongly to each other than a bead is along its own length, so parts split more easily between layers.

From idea to object

model (STL or 3MF) → slicer → G-code → printer

Model
A closed 3D surface made of triangles. Lessons 2 and 3 generate these from code.
Slicer
Bambu Studio, for this printer. It cuts the model into layers and plans the path of the nozzle for each: outlines, fill, supports, travel moves.
G-code
The slicer's output: a plain text list of moves and settings, one per line. Lessons 4 and 5 read, modify and generate it.
Printer
Executes the G-code exactly, line by line. It does not know what the object is.

What a slicer does to your model

Each layer is a horizontal cross-section of the model. The slicer finds its outline and then fills it in three ways:

  • Walls (perimeters): two or more beads tracing the outline. They give the surface its finish and most of the strength.
  • Infill: a sparse pattern inside, typically 10 to 20 per cent solid, that supports the layers above.
  • Top and bottom skins: several fully solid layers where the object's surface is horizontal.

The example slices a simple shape defined by a distance function (Lesson 9). Drag the slider to move the cutting plane up through it. Walls are the band just inside the outline, and the infill lines switch direction on alternate layers so that they cross.

Layers and overhangs

Layer height is a trade. Thin layers (0.08 to 0.12 mm) hide the stair-steps on sloped surfaces and take much longer. Thick layers (0.24 to 0.28 mm) are fast and visibly stepped. The default is 0.2 mm.

Layer height also decides how far a wall can lean. Each layer can overhang the one below by a fraction of its own bead width before the plastic has nothing to stick to. The working rule is that surfaces may lean up to about 45° from vertical without support. The side view below shows a leaning wall as stacked beads. Beads with too little under them turn red.

Try it: set the lean to 55° and then reduce the layer height. Thinner layers shift less per layer, so the same angle becomes printable. That is a real and useful trick.

For a procedural designer these are constraints to build into the generator, exactly as in Special Topic 3. A good generator cannot produce an unprintable object.

How much plastic, how fast

One number ties geometry to the machine: the volumetric flow rate, the volume of plastic leaving the nozzle each second.

flow (mm³/s) = bead width × layer height × speed

A hotend can only melt plastic so fast. Each filament profile in Bambu Studio has a "max volumetric speed" for this reason, and the slicer slows down to stay under it. It is why a printer capable of 500 mm/s usually prints much slower, and it will be the main safety check on any G-code you generate yourself. The example shows the cross-section of a bead and the flow it demands.

Key ideas

  • A print is stacked layers, each drawn with one bead of fixed width.
  • The P1S prints within 256 × 256 mm and up to 250 mm high, except a small front-left corner.
  • You can generate the model, per-layer G-code snippets, or the entire toolpath. Control and responsibility rise together.
  • Overhangs beyond about 45° need support. Thinner layers lean further.
  • Volumetric flow, width × height × speed, is the machine's real speed limit.

Practice

Before the next lesson

  • Read a slice. Open any model in Bambu Studio, slice it, and drag the layer slider in the preview. Find the walls, infill, top skin, supports and seam. Colour the preview by speed, then by flow.
  • Find the limit. In the filament settings, find the "max volumetric speed" for your filament. Use example P1.3 to work out the fastest a 0.42 × 0.2 mm bead can be printed.
  • Break a rule on purpose. Print a small test piece with overhangs at 30°, 45°, 60° and 70°, and look at the undersides. Knowing where your own printer and filament give up is worth more than any guideline.
