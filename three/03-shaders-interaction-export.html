three.js 3

Shaders, Interaction and Export

Three things turn a generated 3D scene into finished work: shaders, which move the procedural logic onto the graphics card; interaction, which lets a person reach into the scene; and export, which takes the result out of the browser to a printer or another program.

Your own shaders in three.js

Lesson 9 wrote fragment shaders: a colour for each pixel. A 3D object adds a second kind. The vertex shader runs once per vertex and decides where that vertex appears, which means it can also move it. Displacing every vertex along its normal by a noise value turns a plain sphere into a living blob, and because it runs on the graphics card, a very dense mesh animates at full speed.

A ShaderMaterial takes both shaders as text. three.js supplies each vertex's position and normal and the camera matrices. You pass in your own values as uniforms, and hand data from the vertex shader to the fragment shader through varyings, which are blended across each triangle.

The lighting here is rough, because it uses the sphere's original normals rather than those of the displaced surface. Fixing that properly means computing the gradient of the displacement (Maths 3). For most stylised work the rough version is fine.

Reaching into the scene

On a flat canvas the mouse position is a position in the picture. In 3D, a point on the screen corresponds to a whole line running away from the camera. Raycasting finds what that line hits. A Raycaster takes the pointer position and the camera, and returns either the objects under the pointer or, as here, the point where the line meets a mathematical plane.

With that 3D point in hand, interaction is ordinary procedural logic. Each column below rises according to a bell curve of its distance from the pointer (Maths 1), and eases toward its target (Special Topic 1).

To find which object is under the pointer instead, call raycaster.intersectObjects(scene.children). It returns the hits nearest first, each with the object, the exact point, and for an instanced mesh the number of the copy that was hit.

The printing lessons built meshes triangle by triangle. three.js offers a shortcut for objects with an axis: LatheGeometry spins a 2D profile round the vertical axis. If the profile is a closed outline of the object's cross-section, from the centre of the base, up the outside, over the rim, down the inside and back to the centre, the result is a watertight solid with real wall thickness.

Two things to remember when exporting for print. three.js has Y up and slicers expect Z up, so rotate the geometry a quarter turn first. And the units of your scene are taken as millimetres.

Keeping it fast

  • Draw calls matter more than triangles. A thousand separate meshes are slower than one mesh with a million triangles. Instance or merge.
  • Do per-vertex work in shaders, not in JavaScript loops.
  • Reuse geometries, materials and helper objects. Creating objects every frame causes stutter.
  • Dispose of geometries and materials you remove: geometry.dispose(). The graphics card's memory is not freed automatically.
  • Cap the pixel ratio at 2. Phones report 3 or more, which triples the work for little gain.
  • Stop rendering when nothing changes or the canvas is off screen.

Key ideas

  • A vertex shader places each vertex and can displace it. Uniforms carry values in, varyings pass them to the fragment shader.
  • Raycasting turns a pointer position into a 3D point or a picked object.
  • Once you have that point, interaction is the same rules as in 2D.
  • LatheGeometry on a closed cross-section gives a printable solid with wall thickness.
  • Rotate Y-up to Z-up before exporting for a slicer.
  • Few draw calls, work in shaders, reuse and dispose.

three.js challenge

A Small World

Build a complete procedural 3D scene: a place, not an object. It should be generated from a seed, respond to the visitor, and run smoothly.

Requirements

  • Everything comes from one seed, shown on screen, and changed by a key press.
  • At least one custom geometry built from your own function: terrain, a parametric surface, or a lathe profile.
  • At least one instanced mesh with a thousand or more copies placed by rule, not uniformly at random.
  • At least one custom shader, however small.
  • Interaction through raycasting: something in the scene responds to where the pointer is.
  • A deliberate palette, lighting and fog, and a camera position you chose, not the default.
  • It holds 60 frames per second on your machine, and resizes with the window.
  • A key saves a PNG. Stretch: export part of it as STL or glTF.

Hints

  • Decide the mood first: a time of day and three colours. Build the lighting before the geometry.
  • Place instances with Poisson-disc sampling (Lesson 7) or a noise threshold, so that they cluster believably.
  • Measure the frame rate with renderer.info.render.calls and the browser's performance tools before optimising.

You are done when

  • Ten seeds give ten scenes you would screenshot.
  • Someone can explore it for a minute and find the interaction without being told.
