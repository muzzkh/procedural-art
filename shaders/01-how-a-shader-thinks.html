Shaders 1

How a Shader Thinks

Lesson 9 introduced shaders through distance fields. This section treats them as a craft of their own. It starts with what the graphics card actually does, the handful of functions every shader is built from, and the habits that make shader code readable and its edges clean.

What the graphics card does

A graphics card draws triangles. For each triangle it runs two small programs that you supply.

  1. The vertex shader runs once for each corner, and says where on the screen that corner goes.
  2. The card then works out which pixels the triangle covers.
  3. The fragment shader runs once for each of those pixels, and says what colour it is.

The examples in this section draw two triangles that together cover the canvas, with a vertex shader that does nothing interesting. Everything happens in the fragment shader, so it runs once for every pixel of the canvas, every frame. three.js 3 shows the other half, where the vertex shader moves the corners of a real model.

The card runs thousands of copies of the fragment shader at the same time. That is where the speed comes from, and it is paid for with three restrictions which shape everything else:

  • No memory. A shader cannot keep a variable from one pixel to the next, or from one frame to the next. Shaders 4 shows the one way round this.
  • No neighbours. A pixel cannot ask what colour the pixel beside it turned out to be.
  • No order. You cannot draw one thing and then another on top. Every pixel must work out for itself everything that might be visible there.

So a shader is a function from position to colour, exactly like the texture functions of the Textures section. The idea of letting artists write that function is forty years old: Robert Cook's "Shade Trees" (SIGGRAPH 1984) described shading as a tree of small operations, and Ken Perlin's "An Image Synthesizer" (SIGGRAPH 1985) gave a programming language that ran at every pixel. GLSL is the descendant that runs in your browser.

What you are given

NameTypeMeaning
gl_FragCoord.xyvec2This pixel's position in pixels. The origin is the bottom left, and y goes up.
u_resvec2The canvas size in pixels
u_timefloatSeconds since the example started
u_mousevec2The pointer's position in pixels, in the same bottom-left coordinates
u_downfloat1.0 while the mouse button is held, otherwise 0.0
gl_FragColorvec4What you must set: red, green, blue and alpha, each from 0 to 1

The names beginning u_ are uniforms: values handed in from JavaScript that are the same for every pixel in a frame. The first line of nearly every shader turns the pixel position into coordinates that are easier to think in. There are two common choices.

vec2 uv = gl_FragCoord.xy / u_res;                       // 0 to 1 across and up the canvas
vec2 p  = (gl_FragCoord.xy * 2.0 - u_res) / u_res.y;     // centred: y runs from -1 to 1, and circles stay round

A shader has no console.log. The only output is the colour, so the first skill is to look at numbers as colours.

Black at the bottom left, red at the bottom right, green at the top left, yellow where both are 1. Replace the first colour line with vec3(fract(uv * 5.0), 0.0) and you can see cells.

The language in brief

GLSL looks like JavaScript with types. The differences that catch everyone:

RuleExample
Every variable has a declared typefloat d = 0.5; vec3 col = vec3(1.0, 0.5, 0.0);
Whole numbers and decimals do not mix. Write the decimal point.x * 2.0, never x * 2
Vectors do arithmetic on all their parts at oncecol * 0.5, a + b, sin(p)
Parts of a vector can be picked and reordered by namep.x, p.yx, col.rgb, col.bgr
One number can fill a whole vectorvec3(0.2) is vec3(0.2, 0.2, 0.2)
Vectors can be built from smaller onesvec4(col, 1.0), vec3(uv, 0.0)
Loops must have a fixed number of turnsfor (int i = 0; i < 5; i++)
A function must be written above the place it is usedHelpers first, main last

The shaping functions

Shaders are built from a very small vocabulary, used again and again. Maths 1 covered these as curves. Here they are as tools.

FunctionWhat it is for
step(edge, x)A hard threshold: 0 below the edge, 1 above. The shader's if.
smoothstep(a, b, x)A soft threshold, rising from 0 to 1 between a and b
mix(a, b, t)Blend from a to b. Works on colours. The shader's way of putting one thing on top of another.
fract(x)Repeat: the position within a cell. floor(x) is which cell.
abs(x)Mirror. abs(fract(x) - 0.5) is a triangle wave.
clamp(x, lo, hi), min, maxLimit a value. min and max also combine shapes (Lesson 9).
length(v), distance(a, b)Distance, and so circles
dot(a, b)How far two directions agree. Lighting, and projections.
sin, cos, atan(y, x)Waves, rotation, and the angle of a point
pow(x, n), exp(x)Sharpen a falloff. Glows and highlights.

The plotter below draws any function of one variable as a curve. It is itself a shader, and its one trick is worth learning now. fwidth(v) tells you how much the value v changes between this pixel and the next. Dividing any value by its fwidth therefore converts it into pixels. So abs(v) / fwidth(v) is "how many pixels am I from the place where v is zero", for any v at all, and that is a line exactly as wide as you ask for.

Clean edges

A hard step gives stair-stepped edges, and where a pattern becomes finer than the pixels it gives the false patterns described in Textures 1. The cure is the same fwidth idea. Instead of asking "is the value above zero?", ask "how many pixels above zero is it?" and ramp from 0 to 1 over one pixel.

float hard = step(0.0, v);
float soft = smoothstep(-1.0, 1.0, v / fwidth(v));

The second line has a useful side effect. Where the pattern is much finer than a pixel, fwidth(v) is large, the ramp never gets far from the middle, and the result settles to an even grey, which is the correct average. Polar coordinates

A point can be described by its distance from the centre and its angle, instead of by x and y (Lesson 4). In a shader that is two built-in functions: length(p) and atan(p.y, p.x). Every tool that worked on x and y now works on distance and angle.

  • A radius that depends on the angle makes flowers, gears and stars.
  • fract of the angle makes cells around a circle: rays, tick marks, slices.
  • Angle plus distance makes spirals.

The angle jumps from π to −π on the left-hand side. Patterns stay seamless there as long as they repeat a whole number of times round the circle.

Layers without drawing order

On a canvas you paint the sky, then the hills, then the tree. A shader cannot. Each pixel has to reach the same result alone, and the idiom for it is a chain of mix calls. Start with the colour of the furthest thing. For each nearer thing, work out a mask, a number from 0 to 1 saying how much of it covers this pixel, and mix its colour over what you have so far.

vec3 col = background;
col = mix(col, colourOfThing1, maskOfThing1);
col = mix(col, colourOfThing2, maskOfThing2);     // nearer, so later

Light that glows is the exception. It is added to the colour instead of mixed over it, because a glow does not hide what is behind it.

Debugging by colour

When a shader shows something unexpected, the fault is nearly always a number that is not what you believed: negative when you assumed positive, in the hundreds when you assumed 0 to 1. The screen clips everything to 0 to 1, so those all look black or white. A small function that draws a number honestly saves hours.

The inspect function below shows values from 0 to 1 as grey, negative values as blue, values above 1 as red, and adds a contour line every quarter. The value being inspected is a distance field that has been multiplied by 3. Displayed directly, it would be a black disc on a white square with no hint of the scale.

Keep inspect in a file of your own and paste it into any shader that misbehaves. Other quick checks: output vec4(fract(uv * 10.0), 0.0, 1.0) to see whether your coordinates are what you think, and output a direction as v * 0.5 + 0.5 to see it as a colour, which is how a normal map works (Textures 4).

Key ideas

  • A fragment shader runs once per pixel, in parallel, with no memory, no neighbours and no drawing order.
  • Turn gl_FragCoord into convenient coordinates on the first line.
  • A dozen built-in functions do nearly everything: step, smoothstep, mix, fract, abs, length, dot and a few more.
  • fwidth(v) is how much v changes per pixel. Dividing by it turns any value into pixels, which gives lines of a fixed width and edges exactly one pixel soft.
  • length and atan give polar coordinates. Everything that works on x and y works on distance and angle.
  • Layers are a chain of mix calls, from the furthest thing to the nearest.
  • To debug, show the number as a colour that cannot hide its range.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that y points up. A shader ported from canvas code comes out upside down.
  • Dividing by u_res when you wanted round circles. Divide both coordinates by u_res.y.
  • Using if for shapes. It works, but it gives hard edges. A mask and a mix give soft ones.
  • Calling fwidth on something that jumps, such as fract(x) itself. Apply it to a value that is continuous, such as the triangle wave abs(fract(x) - 0.5).

Shaders challenge 1

A Stopwatch

Draw a stopwatch face, with a hand that sweeps once a minute driven by u_time, entirely in one fragment shader. It is a small object, but it uses everything in this lesson and leaves nowhere for a rough edge to hide.

Requirements

  • A round face with a rim, sixty tick marks with every fifth one longer, and a seconds hand. A second, slower hand or a small inner dial counts minutes.
  • The tick marks come from fract of the angle, not from sixty separate shapes.
  • Everything is assembled as layers with mix, in a clear order from back to front.
  • Every edge is one pixel soft, using fwidth or a smoothstep of known width. Check it at two canvas sizes.
  • The hand's angle is exact: at 15 seconds it points to the right, at 30 straight down.
  • A comment at the top lists the layers in order.

Hints

  • Rotate the point instead of the hand. Turn p by the hand's angle, then draw a hand that always points straight up.
  • A straight hand is a rectangle: the distance to a line segment, or simply two smoothstep masks multiplied together.
  • When something looks wrong, show the angle with inspect(a / 6.2832) and read it.

Stretch goals

  • Make the hand tick: move in sixty steps with a small overshoot at each, using floor, fract and an easing curve (Lesson 15).
  • Add numerals at 15, 30, 45 and 60 built from segments.
  • Give the glass a soft highlight that is added, not mixed.

You are done when

  • The face looks equally clean at 300 and at 1000 pixels.
  • You can change the number of ticks by editing one number.
