Shaders 3

Raymarching and Light

Lesson 9 ended with a glimpse of a 3D scene drawn by a single fragment shader. This lesson builds that method properly: a camera, the march, surface normals, and then the lighting that makes a distance function look like a solid object. Light, shadow, occlusion and fog each turn out to be a few lines.

The method

There are no triangles in these scenes. The whole scene is one function, map(p), which takes a point in space and returns the distance from it to the nearest surface (Lesson 9). To find what a pixel sees:

  1. Stand at the camera and face along the ray that passes through this pixel.
  2. Ask map how far the nearest surface is. Nothing can be closer than that in any direction, so it is safe to move that far along the ray.
  3. Repeat from the new position. The steps shrink as a surface approaches.
  4. Stop when the distance is tiny (a hit), or when you have gone too far (a miss).

John Hart described and analysed this in "Sphere Tracing: A Geometric Method for the Antialiased Ray Tracing of Implicit Surfaces" (The Visual Computer, 1996). The name comes from step 2: at each stop there is an empty sphere around you, and you walk to its edge. Printing 3 uses the same distance functions to make models for a printer.

A camera

A camera needs a position, called the eye, and a point to look at. From those two, three directions follow, each at right angles to the others, built with the cross product (Maths 2 and Maths 6):

  • forward: from the eye toward the target;
  • right: at right angles to forward and to the world's "up";
  • up: at right angles to both of those.

The ray for a pixel is forward, plus right times the pixel's x, plus up times the pixel's y. The number that multiplies forward sets the angle of view: larger is a longer lens.

Seeing the march

The first example is a ball on the ground with no lighting at all. Left of the pointer, the colour is the surface normal shown as a colour, as in a normal map (Textures 4). The normal of a distance function is its gradient, the direction in which the distance grows fastest, and it is measured by sampling map a tiny step either side along each axis (Maths 3).

Right of the pointer, the colour is how many steps the march took: dark for few, bright for many. This is the cost of the picture. Rays that hit something head-on are cheap. Rays that skim past an edge, or run nearly level with the ground toward the horizon, take many small steps.

Light

With a position and a normal for the visible point, shading is the arithmetic of Textures 4, now in three dimensions.

Diffuse
The dot product of the normal and the direction to the light, cut off at zero. The matt part.
Specular
The dot product of the normal and the direction halfway between the light and the eye, raised to a high power. The highlight.
Ambient
A small constant, standing in for light bouncing in from everywhere else, so that unlit sides are not pure black.

One more line matters. Light adds up in proportion to energy, but a screen's numbers are not proportional to energy: a stored value of 0.5 emits only about a fifth of the light of 1.0. So do the lighting sums as if the numbers were energy, then convert at the very end by raising the colour to the power 1/2.2. Without that step shadows are too dark and the lit side burns out. Lesson 11 covers the same curve from the side of colour.

Move the pointer left and right to orbit, and up and down to raise the camera.

Shadows

The scene above has light and dark sides but nothing casts a shadow, because each point was shaded as if it were alone. To find out whether a point is in shadow, march again: start at the point and walk toward the light. If the march hits something, the light is blocked.

That gives a hard-edged shadow. Real shadows are soft-edged, because real lights have a size. The march already holds the information needed to fake this. A ray that reaches the light but passes very close to an object on the way would be partly blocked if the light were larger. So keep track of the nearest miss, relative to how far along the ray it happened, and darken in proportion. The cost is the same loop.

Occlusion, fog and sky

Three more short pieces finish a basic renderer.

Ambient occlusion
Textures 4 darkened hollows by comparing a height with its blurred surroundings. With a distance function the test is direct. Step a little way out from the surface along the normal and ask map for the distance. In open space the answer equals the distance you stepped. If it is less, something else is nearby, and less of the sky's light reaches the point. Five samples are enough.
Fog
Blend the surface colour toward the sky colour by an amount that grows with distance. It gives depth, and it hides the far cut-off of the march.
Sky
What a ray sees when it hits nothing: a colour that depends on the ray's direction.

The scene is lit only by ambient light, so that the occlusion is easy to see. Left of the pointer it is off.

Several materials

So far every object has been the same colour, apart from a test on height for the floor. For a scene with several materials, let map return two numbers: the distance, and a number identifying which object that distance belongs to. A small helper keeps the nearer of two such pairs. After the march, the identity picks the colour and the shininess.

This example also uses the two combining operations from Lesson 9 in 3D. The bowl is a hollow sphere with its top cut away, using max. The pear is two balls melted together with a smooth minimum. Everything from this lesson is switched on: diffuse light, a highlight, soft shadows, occlusion, fog and the final conversion.

Repeating space

Lesson 9 repeated a 2D shape across the plane by taking fract of the coordinates before drawing it. The same works in 3D and is the most surprising property of the method. Wrap two of the coordinates with mod, and one column becomes an endless hall of them at no extra cost: the march only ever measures the distance to the nearest copy.

Because the hall repeats every four units, the camera's position can be wrapped every four units as well. The view after four units of travel is identical to the view at the start, so the flight loops perfectly (Lesson 15) and the numbers stay small.

What it costs, and when not to use it

Every pixel runs the march, up to a hundred calls to map, then six more for the normal, then a shadow march of up to sixty, then five for occlusion. A map of ten objects makes that well over a thousand distance calculations per pixel. Three habits keep it usable:

  • Keep map cheap. It is called more than anything else. Test a simple bounding shape first, and only evaluate the detail when the point is near it.
  • Stop early. A far limit and a hit threshold that grows with distance both save steps where nobody can see the difference.
  • Draw fewer pixels. These examples are 560 by 400 for that reason.

Raymarching suits soft, blended, repeating and mathematical forms, and scenes that are a single idea. It is a poor fit for scenes made of many separate, detailed objects, for anything that needs to be exported as a model, and for anything that must run on a phone at full resolution. For those, build triangles (three.js 2) and use shaders to colour them (three.js 3).

Key ideas

  • A scene is a function that returns the distance to the nearest surface. The march steps along a ray by that distance until it arrives.
  • A camera is an eye, a target, and three directions at right angles built with cross products.
  • The normal is the gradient of the distance function, measured by small differences.
  • Light is diffuse plus specular plus a little ambient, converted for the screen at the end.
  • A shadow is a second march, toward the light. The nearest miss along the way gives a soft edge for free.
  • Occlusion compares a short step along the normal with the distance found there.
  • map can return an identity as well as a distance, to choose materials.
  • mod on the coordinates repeats an object for ever at no extra cost.

Common mistakes

  • A map that is not a true distance, because a shape was stretched or twisted. The march oversteps and surfaces show holes or banding. Multiply the result by a number below 1 to take smaller, safer steps.
  • Starting the shadow march exactly on the surface. It hits the surface it started from. Move the start a little way along the normal.
  • Black speckles on surfaces lit at a shallow angle. The hit threshold is too coarse for the normal's step, or the reverse. Keep them the same size.
  • Objects that cross the boundaries of a repeated cell. They are cut off. Keep each copy inside its cell.

Shaders challenge 3

A Still Life in One Shader

Compose and light a small scene of objects on a surface, as a painter would set up a still life, in one fragment shader.

Requirements

  • At least four objects of at least three different kinds, on a surface, arranged deliberately: something tall, something low, something overlapping something else.
  • At least one object made by cutting one shape from another, and one made by a smooth blend.
  • At least three materials that differ in more than colour: at least one matt and one glossy.
  • Diffuse light, a highlight, soft shadows and occlusion, each in its own function, with the screen conversion at the end.
  • One of the surfaces carries a procedural texture from Shaders 2, such as wood grain on the table or speckle on a fruit.
  • A camera that the pointer moves, or a slow automatic move that loops.

Hints

  • Set up the composition with flat grey materials and one light first. Colour comes last.
  • A warm main light with cooler ambient light, or the reverse, makes forms read more clearly than a single white light.
  • When a shape looks wrong, show the step count, as in SH3.1. A bright patch means map is misbehaving there.
  • To texture an object, use its own coordinates: the hit position minus the object's centre.

Stretch goals

  • A second, weaker light of a different colour.
  • A mirror-like object: when the march hits it, reflect the ray about the normal and march once more.

You are done when

  • Every object visibly rests on something: no floating, no gaps under bases.
  • A stranger can say what each object is made of.
