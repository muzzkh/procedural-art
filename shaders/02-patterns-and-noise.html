Shaders 2

Patterns and Noise on the Graphics Card

Everything in the Textures section was a function of position, which is exactly what a shader is. This lesson moves those ideas to the graphics card, where they run fast enough to animate: cells and hashes, tiles, the two kinds of noise, and the materials built from them.

What changes when you move to GLSL

The ideas do not change. Three practical things do.

  • There is no random number generator and no noise function. You write a hash and build noise on it. This is a few lines that you will paste into the top of almost every shader.
  • Time is free. The whole picture is recomputed every frame anyway, so anything can depend on u_time at no extra cost.
  • Numbers are less precise. A graphics card's float has about seven significant digits, against sixteen in JavaScript. Keep the numbers you hash small, and do not let u_time multiply into the thousands inside a sin.

Cells and a hash

As in Textures 1, floor gives the cell and fract the position inside it. The hash is the well-known one-liner from Lesson 9: a dot product to combine the two cell numbers, a sine, a large multiplier, and fract to keep the scrambled low digits. Adding a constant to the cell number before hashing gives a second, unrelated random number for the same cell.

This example also shows a limit of fwidth from Shaders 1. Each cell's shape has its own random size, so the distance value jumps at cell borders, and fwidth reports a huge change there. When you know the scale of your coordinates it is safer to work out the size of a pixel yourself. Here the canvas is ten units tall, so one pixel is 10.0 / u_res.y units.

Tiles that turn

Truchet tiles (Lesson 2) are a single tile laid in one of two orientations chosen at random. In a shader the orientation is a hash of the cell, and turning the tile is done by mirroring the position inside the cell before drawing. This is the general method for per-cell variation: change the coordinates, then draw the same thing.

The tile holds two quarter-circles centred on opposite corners. Because every tile's curves meet its edges at their midpoints, any arrangement joins up into unbroken paths. Here each cell is rolled again from time to time, by adding a slowly counting whole number to what is hashed.

Two kinds of lattice noise

Lesson 9 used value noise: a random number at each whole-number position, blended smoothly in between. It is the simplest noise, and it has a flaw you can see once you look for it. The peaks and hollows sit exactly on the lattice, so the result has a faint squareness.

Gradient noise, which is what Perlin's noise is, stores a random direction at each lattice point instead of a random value. Each corner of a cell says "I am zero here, and I rise in this direction". The four corners' opinions are blended. The value is zero at every lattice point, the features fall between them, and the squareness largely disappears.

The two are shown side by side with contour bands, which make the difference easier to see. Move the pointer to move the split.

Octaves, and a surface with light on it

fBm is the same loop as before. One refinement is standard in shaders: rotate the coordinates a little between octaves. Without it, every octave's lattice lines up with the one below, and their faint grids reinforce each other. A fixed rotation matrix costs almost nothing and hides them.

With a height available at any point, Textures 4 applies directly: take the slope by sampling the height a small step away in x and in y, build a normal, and light it. Below, the height is also passed through a colour ramp to make a map, anything under the water level is made flat and glossy, and the pointer is the lamp.

Cell noise with moving points

Worley's cell noise (Textures 2) is a short loop over the nine cells around the pixel. In a shader the points can move. Each one circles inside its own cell, driven by u_time and its own hash, and the whole diagram rearranges itself continuously. Keeping each point inside its cell matters: it is what guarantees that the nearest point is always among the nine cells searched.

Putting it together: light through water

The bright network on the floor of a swimming pool is called a caustic. It is expensive to compute physically and easy to imitate, which is the usual situation in shader writing. Two observations do it. The pattern looks like the walls of a cell diagram. And it shifts and breaks up, which suggests two such diagrams sliding across each other, each bent by slow noise.

Every part of this has appeared above: cell noise, value noise used as a warp (Lesson 3), a sharp falloff from exp, and light that is added instead of mixed.

Keeping it fast

A shader's cost is what one pixel costs, multiplied by the number of pixels, sixty times a second. A canvas of 1200 by 1200 real pixels is 86 million shader runs each second. The habits that matter:

  • Count the noise calls. One fBm of six octaves is six noise calls, each four hashes. A warp built from three fBms, sampled three times for a slope, is 216 hashes per pixel. Use fewer octaves for the warp than for the final value: nobody can see fine detail in a displacement.
  • Loops have a fixed length, so an early break is the only way to save work inside one.
  • Draw fewer pixels. Halving the canvas's width and height quarters the work, and soft, noisy pictures survive being scaled up.

Lesson 19 covers measuring. The same rule applies here: measure before you optimise.

Key ideas

  • A shader has no random function. A hash of the cell number supplies repeatable randomness.
  • To vary cells, change the coordinates inside the cell, then draw the same thing.
  • Value noise stores a random value at each lattice point. Gradient noise stores a random direction and is less square.
  • Rotating the coordinates between octaves hides the lattice.
  • A height function, sampled twice more for its slope, can be lit like any height map.
  • Cell noise is a search of nine cells, and its points can move as long as each stays in its own cell.
  • Cost is per pixel. Count noise calls.

Common mistakes

  • Hashing large or fast-growing numbers. The pattern breaks into stripes or blocks, differently on different machines. Hash cell numbers, and keep them below a few thousand.
  • Using fwidth across cell borders, where values jump. Work out the pixel size from your own scale instead.
  • Adding time to the coordinates of noise and calling it animation. That only slides the pattern. To make it change shape, move two layers differently, or warp one with another.

Shaders challenge 2

A Living Texture

Take one material from your Textures 2 board, or choose a new surface that moves in life (water, fire, cloud, a field of grass in wind, embers), and write it as a single fragment shader that never stops changing.

Requirements

  • Built from a hash you pasted in and noise functions you can explain line by line.
  • Uses both lattice noise and cell noise, or lattice noise and a cell pattern from floor and fract.
  • It changes shape over time. Sliding the whole pattern sideways does not count.
  • It is lit: a normal from the slope of a height, a light at the pointer, and a highlight where the material would have one.
  • A colour ramp of at least three stops.
  • It runs smoothly on your machine at full canvas size. Write the number of hashes per pixel in a comment.

Hints

  • Write the JavaScript version's texture(u, v) into GLSL one line at a time. Most lines need only types added.
  • Two layers moving at different speeds in different directions read as one changing thing.
  • Use inspect from Shaders 1 to look at the height on its own before lighting it.

Stretch goals

  • Make it loop perfectly every ten seconds (Lesson 15).
  • Let the pointer disturb it as well as light it.

You are done when

  • Someone watching for thirty seconds cannot find the moment it repeats or the direction it is sliding.
  • They can name the material.
