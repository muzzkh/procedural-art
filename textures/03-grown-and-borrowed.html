Textures 3

Grown and Borrowed Textures

Not every texture can be written as a formula of its coordinates. Some are easier to grow with a simulation, and some are easier to borrow from a small example and extend. This lesson covers both, and the problem they share: covering a large area without visible repetition.

Three ways to get a texture

MethodYou supplyGood forCost
Formula (Textures 1 and 2)A function of positionAnything you can describe: bricks, wood, marbleCheap. Every point is independent.
SimulationRules and a starting stateAnimal coats, corals, corrosion, anything that formed by a processMany steps over a whole grid before you have a result

The last two give up the property that made formulas convenient. Their value at a point depends on other points, so they are computed once into an image and stored. What they give in return is detail you did not have to design.

Grown: reaction and diffusion

In 1952 Alan Turing proposed, in "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis", that the spots and stripes of animals could arise from two chemicals that react with each other while spreading at different speeds. You built such a system in Lesson 8. Two papers presented side by side at SIGGRAPH 1991 turned it into a texturing method: Greg Turk's "Generating Textures on Arbitrary Surfaces Using Reaction-Diffusion", and "Reaction-Diffusion Textures" by Andrew Witkin and Michael Kass. Between them they added the three ideas that make it useful as a design tool.

  • Parameters that vary across the surface. If the feed rate changes from place to place, so does the pattern. Below it is low at the sides and high in the middle, so spots merge into a maze. This is how one animal has spots on its legs and stripes on its tail.
  • Diffusion with a direction. If the chemicals spread faster one way than the other, the pattern stretches that way. Use the slider. Witkin and Kass called this anisotropy and used it to make stripes follow a chosen direction.
  • A grid that wraps round. If the left edge's neighbours are the right edge, the finished texture tiles with no further work.

Give it a minute, then move the slider and watch the markings realign. Press Run to start again. A grown texture is slow to make and cannot be resized by changing one number, so in practice it is run once, saved as an image, and used as the input to something else: a mask for Textures 2, or a height for Textures 4.

The repetition problem

Any stored texture, whether grown, painted or photographed, is a finite tile. Repeat it across a floor and the eye finds the repeat at once, because the same distinctive mark turns up at regular intervals. A seamless tile (Textures 1) hides the joins but not the rhythm. The rest of this lesson is three answers from the research literature, in order of age.

Wang tiles: a small set that never settles into a rhythm

Suppose you had several square tiles instead of one, with coloured edges, and the rule that two tiles may sit side by side only if the touching edges have the same colour. If every colour is designed to join seamlessly to itself, any legal arrangement is seamless. If there are at least two legal choices at every position and you pick at random, the arrangement never repeats. Michael Cohen, Jonathan Shade, Stefan Hiller and Oliver Deussen described this for graphics in "Wang Tiles for Image and Texture Generation" (ACM Transactions on Graphics, 2003). It is the same matching rule as Wave Function Collapse (Lesson 14), used here in its simplest form, where the left-to-right order makes contradictions impossible.

How do you make tiles whose edges match? In the example the material is cobbles, and each tile is a four-by-four arrangement of stones. Where a stone sits is decided by what the tile's edges are:

  • The stones along an edge are placed by that edge's colour and nothing else. Two tiles that meet at an edge of the same colour therefore agree about every stone on both sides of the join.
  • The four corner stones are the same in every tile, because four tiles meet at a corner and all must agree.
  • The stones in the middle are placed by the tile's whole combination of edge colours, so each of the sixteen tiles has its own interior.

The result is that every tile's picture depends only on its own four colours, so the sixteen tiles could be drawn once and stored as images.

Set the first slider to 0 and the grid of repeats is obvious. Set it to 1 and a small amount of stored material covers the area with no rhythm. Sixteen tiles can be stored once and laid out by a hash of the tile position (Textures 1), so the method works per pixel, on a graphics card, over any area.

Turn on the edge colours and look at where four tiles meet. The stone there is in the same place every time. This is the known weakness of edge-coloured tiles: the corners are shared by everything. Later work uses tiles with coloured corners instead of coloured edges to avoid it.

Borrowed: growing a texture from an example

Alexei Efros and Thomas Leung's "Texture Synthesis by Non-parametric Sampling" (ICCV 1999) asked a different question. Given a small sample, can a program produce more of the same, with no model of what the texture is? Their answer is short enough to state in full.

  1. To choose the colour of the next pixel, look at the pixels already made around it.
  2. Find every place in the sample where the surroundings look similar.
  3. Pick one of those places at random and copy the pixel found there.

The version below fills the output in reading order, so the surroundings are the three rows above and the three pixels to the left. The sample is the small square on the left: an irregular brick pattern. Nothing in the code knows about bricks. The structure survives because every pixel is chosen to continue what is already there.

Change reach to 1 and run it again. The neighbourhood is now too small to see a whole brick, and the result loses its structure. The size of the neighbourhood must match the size of the largest feature you want to keep. That is the method's one real parameter, and also its cost: it compares every output pixel with the entire sample.

Borrowed in patches: quilting

Two years later Efros and William Freeman replaced pixels with patches, in "Image Quilting for Texture Synthesis and Transfer" (SIGGRAPH 2001). Copying a whole block at a time keeps the structure inside the block for free, and is far faster. The difficulty moves to the joins. The paper presents the method in three stages, and the slider steps through them.

0. Random blocks
Take blocks from anywhere in the sample and lay them edge to edge. Every join shows.
1. Matched blocks
Let neighbouring blocks overlap by a narrow strip. For each position, try many candidate blocks and keep the one that agrees best with what is already there in the overlap. The joins are better, but still straight lines.
2. Matched and cut
Inside the overlap, do not switch from old to new along a straight line. Find the path along which the two differ least and switch there. The path is found by the same row-by-row search you would use for the cheapest route down a grid of costs. The join now runs between stones instead of through them.

Blending without washing out

A third route is the cheapest of all: lay copies of the sample over the surface at random offsets and blend between them. No search and no stored output. The catch is that averaging two unrelated pieces of a texture gives something greyer than either. Contrast drops wherever copies are being mixed, and the surface shows soft blotches on the same grid as the blend.

Eric Heitz and Fabrice Neyret explained and fixed this in "High-Performance By-Example Noise using a Histogram-Preserving Blending Operator" (2018). Averaging values with weights that add up to 1 shrinks their spread by a predictable amount. So measure each value as a difference from the sample's average colour, blend the differences, divide by the square root of the sum of the squared weights, which is exactly the amount of shrinkage, and add the average back. The third panel does that.

What this example leaves out. The paper does two more things. It blends three copies on a triangular grid instead of four on a square one, and before blending it reshapes the sample's values so that they follow a bell curve, undoing that reshaping afterwards. The second step is what makes the result match the sample's full range of values and not only its contrast. The example keeps only the contrast correction, which is the core of the idea. The method suits textures without large structures: stone, sand, rust, grass. It would shred the bricks of TX3.3.

Choosing between them

If the surface hasUse
A structure you can describeA formula (Textures 1 and 2)
A pattern that formed by a processA simulation, run once and stored
Fine, even grain with no large featuresRandom offsets with contrast-preserving blending
Medium features: stones, leaves, weaveQuilting, or Wang tiles built from quilted pieces
Strict structure from a sample: text, brickworkPixel-at-a-time synthesis with a large neighbourhood, or better, a formula

Today, methods based on neural networks do the job of the last two rows with better results on difficult samples, at far greater cost. They are described in Special Topic 6. The methods on this page still matter because they run in a few lines, need no training, and you can see why they produce what they produce.

Key ideas

  • Textures can be written as formulas, grown by simulation, or extended from an example.
  • Reaction–diffusion becomes a design tool when its parameters vary across the surface and its diffusion has a direction.
  • A single tile repeats visibly even when its edges join. A small set of edge-matched tiles laid at random does not.
  • Pixel-at-a-time synthesis copies the pixel whose surroundings in the sample best match what has been made so far.
  • Quilting copies blocks, chooses them by their overlap, and joins them along the path of least difference.
  • Averaging textures lowers contrast by a known amount, which can be put back.

Common mistakes

  • A neighbourhood or block smaller than the texture's largest feature. The feature will not survive.
  • A sample that is too small. If it holds only two of something, the output will show those two over and over.
  • Using a photograph with uneven lighting as a sample. The bright corner is copied along with the texture and reappears as blotches. Even out the lighting first.

Textures challenge 3

An Endless Surface

Start from one small sample of your own, no larger than 128 pixels square, and cover a canvas at least six times as wide with it, twice, by two different methods. Put the two results side by side and say which is better for your sample, and why.

Requirements

  • A sample you made: generated by one of your Textures 2 materials, grown by a simulation, or a photograph you took.
  • Two of these methods, written by you: Wang tiles, pixel-at-a-time synthesis, quilting, or contrast-preserving blending.
  • A third panel showing the sample simply tiled, as the thing to beat.
  • A fixed seed, so that the comparison is repeatable.
  • A short written verdict in a comment: where each method fails on this sample, and what you would change.

Hints

  • To use a photograph, draw it to a canvas and read it with getImageData, as in Lesson 13.
  • For quilting, a block about twice the size of the largest feature and an overlap of a sixth of the block is a reasonable start.
  • Squint, or step back from the screen. Repetition that is invisible close up is obvious from across the room.

Stretch goals

  • Build the sixteen Wang tiles by quilting, so that each tile's interior comes from the sample and only its edges are constrained.
  • Make the output itself tile, so that it can be repeated as well.

You are done when

  • Someone shown only the large surface cannot point to a repeat.
  • Someone shown the sample agrees that the surface is the same material.
