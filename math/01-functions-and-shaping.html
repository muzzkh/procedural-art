Maths 1

Functions and Shaping

A formula is a machine: a number goes in, a number comes out. Nearly every visual decision in procedural art is a choice of which machine to put between a raw value and a drawn one.

Why maths at all

In procedural art you do not draw the picture, you describe it. Mathematics is the language that description is written in. That does not mean proofs or exams. It means a working vocabulary of a few dozen formulas, each of which does something you can see. This section goes through them by what they look like on the canvas.

Almost all of it follows one pipeline:

raw value → normalise to 0–1 → shape → map to what you need

  • Normalise: turn an index, a distance or a time into a fraction t between 0 and 1.
  • Shape: bend that fraction with a function, so that change happens early, late, smoothly or in steps.
  • Map: scale the result to a radius, an angle, a colour or a position.

The first and last steps are bookkeeping. The middle step is where the design happens.

Linear interpolation

lerp(a, b, t) = a + (b − a) · t

Start at a, and add a fraction t of the journey to b. At t = 0 you are at a, at t = 1 at b, at t = 0.5 exactly halfway. It works on anything made of numbers: positions, sizes, angles, each channel of a colour. Its inverse, which recovers the fraction from a value, is the normalising step:

t = (value − a) / (b − a)

PA.map is simply the second formula followed by the first.

Shaping functions

A shaping function takes a fraction from 0 to 1 and returns another fraction from 0 to 1, by a different route. The straight route is linear. The others spend more of the journey near one end.

FormulaBehaviourUse it for
t * t, t ** 3Slow start, fast finishMany small things and a few large; acceleration
Math.sqrt(t), t ** 0.3Fast start, slow finishQuick attack that settles; even density in a disc
1 - (1 - t) ** nThe mirror image of a powerEasing out with a chosen strength
t * t * (3 - 2 * t)Slow at both ends (smoothstep)Soft edges, natural motion, blending noise
0.5 - 0.5 * Math.cos(Math.PI * t)Slow at both ends, from a cosineThe same, and it loops cleanly
Math.floor(t * n) / nA staircase of n stepsPosterised bands, quantised angles

The exponent as a single dial

The power function t ** p is the most useful shaper because one number changes its whole character: below 1 it bulges early, above 1 it bulges late. Here forty rings are placed at radius t ** p while p drifts. The same rings read as a bowl, a flat target, or a tunnel.

Functions that repeat

A second family of functions never ends: they cycle. Feed them a steadily growing input and they produce a pattern.

FormulaWave shapeOn the canvas
x % n, PA.fract(x)Sawtooth: ramps up and snaps backRepetition, wrapping, tiling
PA.fract(x) < 0.5Square: on, offStripes, checks, dashes
Math.abs(PA.fract(x) - 0.5) * 2Triangle: up, downZigzags, folds, ping-pong motion
Math.sin(x)Sine: smooth swingWaves, ripples, breathing

The key move is composition: put one function inside another. A sine of x gives vertical bands. A sine of distance from a point gives rings. The sum of two ring patterns gives interference, the pattern of two stones dropped in a pond. In each quadrant below, one formula is evaluated for every pixel.

Exponentials and logarithms

Linear change adds the same amount at each step. Exponential change multiplies by the same amount at each step.

linear: a, a + d, a + 2d, … exponential: a, a·r, a·r², …

Multiplication by a constant ratio is what growth and decay look like in nature, and it produces self-similarity: every step is a scaled copy of the last. Shells, horns, galaxies and the branches of trees are all built this way. As a continuous formula, decay is Math.exp(-k * t), which you have used for fading and damping.

The example draws a square, then repeatedly rotates by a fixed angle and shrinks by a fixed ratio. The ratio is chosen by a little trigonometry so that each square exactly touches the sides of the one before. The corners trace a logarithmic spiral, the curve whose radius grows exponentially with angle.

Logarithms undo exponentials: they turn multiplying into adding. In practice you reach for Math.log when values span a huge range and you need to see all of them, such as a map of how often each pixel was hit (Maths 4 uses this), or sizes that go from 1 to 10,000.

The bell curve as a brush

bell(d) = e−d² / σ²

The Gaussian function is 1 at distance zero and falls smoothly toward 0, with σ (sigma) setting how wide it is. It is the natural shape for influence: how strongly something affects its surroundings. Use it for soft brushes, glows, magnets, bulges and any effect that should fade out without an edge.

Here it controls how far each point of a mesh is pushed away from the mouse.

Key ideas

  • Normalise, shape, map. The shaping function is the design decision.
  • lerp blends any two values. Its inverse turns a value into a fraction.
  • Powers bend a 0–1 range toward one end. Smoothstep eases both ends.
  • Periodic functions turn a growing number into a repeating pattern.
  • Putting one function inside another is how simple formulas become rich patterns.
  • Adding gives lines and even spacing. Multiplying gives spirals and self-similarity.

Practice

Three exercises

Do these in the maths sandbox file, or by editing the examples above.

  • Shaped grid. Take the grid from Lesson 1 and make the cell spacing uneven: place column i at shape(i / cols) * W. Try three shaping functions and note what each does to the sense of depth.
  • Formula wallpaper. Write a per-pixel pattern using only sin, fract, abs and distance, with at least two functions composed. No loops over shapes, no randomness.
  • Nested polygons. Repeat example M1.4 with triangles or pentagons. Work out the shrink ratio that makes each one touch the last, or find it by trial.
