Maths 3

Calculus

Calculus is the mathematics of change. It asks two questions: how fast is this changing right here, and how much has it added up to so far? In procedural art those become: which way does this curve or surface face, and how far along am I?

Calculus without the algebra

School calculus is mostly rules for manipulating symbols. You need almost none of that here, because a computer can answer both questions numerically, by brute force:

  • The derivative (rate of change): evaluate the function at two points very close together and see how much it changed.
  • The integral (accumulation): chop the thing into many small pieces and add them up.

What you do need is the meaning of each, so that you recognise when a design problem is one or the other.

The derivative: slope

The derivative of a function at a point is its slope there: how much the output changes per unit change of input. Steep uphill is a large positive derivative, flat is zero, downhill is negative.

f′(x) ≈ ( f(x + h) − f(x − h) ) / 2h for a small h

That is called a finite difference. It works on any function at all, including noise, which has no formula you could differentiate by hand.

Below, the top graph is a function and the bottom graph is its derivative. Move the mouse along it. Notice three things: where the top curve peaks or bottoms out, the derivative crosses zero; where the top curve is steepest, the derivative is at its extreme; and the derivative of a wave is another wave, shifted.

Tangents and normals

For a curve drawn from a parameter, (x(t), y(t)), take the derivative of each coordinate. Together they form a vector that points along the curve at that spot: the tangent. Turn it a quarter turn (Maths 2) and you have the normal, pointing straight out sideways.

The normal is one of the most useful things in line-based art. With it you can:

  • give a line a thickness that varies, like a brush or a nib,
  • draw parallel offset curves, like contour lines round a shape,
  • place hatching, bristles or leaves at right angles to a stem,
  • wobble a line sideways without changing where it is going (Lesson 10's sketchy line did this).

The derivative of the derivative, the second derivative, measures how quickly the direction itself is changing. For a curve that is its curvature: large in tight bends, zero on straights. Use it to colour a line by how sharply it turns, to thicken it in the corners, or to slow a moving point through bends as a driver would.

The gradient: slope of a surface

A field (Lesson 1) gives a number for every point of the plane. Picture it as a landscape where the number is height. Now the slope has a direction as well as a size. Measure the slope in x and the slope in y separately, by finite differences, and put the two together as a vector:

∇f = ( ∂f/∂x , ∂f/∂y )

This is the gradient. It points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length says how steep. Three large techniques hang on it.

1. Lighting a surface

A slope facing the light is bright and a slope facing away is dark. So brightness is the dot product (Maths 2) of the gradient with the light direction. This is how terrain maps get their relief, how shaders light a raymarched scene (Lesson 9's normalAt is a gradient), and how to make any noise look three-dimensional.

2. Flowing downhill

Step repeatedly in the direction opposite to the gradient and you roll downhill to the nearest low point. This is gradient descent, the same idea that trains neural networks. On a noise landscape, hundreds of raindrops doing this converge into the valleys and draw a river system that nobody designed.

Try it: change the two minus signs to plus. The drops now climb, and trace the ridgelines instead.

3. Following the contours

Turn the gradient a quarter turn and you get the direction in which height does not change at all: along the contour line. A flow field built this way never converges or spreads out, which is exactly the curl noise of Lesson 5. So the three classic things to do with a noise landscape are the same vector used three ways: along it (ridges), against it (rivers), and across it (contours).

The integral: adding up

Integration is accumulation. If the derivative turns position into speed, the integral turns speed back into position, by adding up many small steps:

total ≈ f(x₀)·h + f(x₁)·h + f(x₂)·h + …

You have been integrating since Lesson 5. The line position += velocity, repeated every frame, is a numerical integral of velocity over time. Maths 4 builds on exactly that.

Arc length

A direct design use is measuring the length of a curve: break it into tiny straight pieces and add their lengths. Why would you want that? Because the parameter of a curve usually does not move at constant speed. Dots placed at equal steps of t bunch up where the parameter moves slowly and spread out where it races. To space things evenly along a path (beads, dashes, text, leaves on a stem) you place them at equal steps of distance instead, which requires the running total of length.

The same running-total trick solves a problem from Lesson 2. To draw random numbers with any distribution you like, add up the distribution to get its running total, then look up a uniform random number in that table. It is called inverse transform sampling, and R.weighted is a small case of it.

Key ideas

  • The derivative is slope. Numerically it is the difference between two nearby values.
  • For a curve, the derivative is the tangent. A quarter turn gives the normal.
  • For a field, the derivative is the gradient: a vector pointing uphill.
  • Along the gradient is ridges, against it is rivers, across it is contours, and its dot product with a light is shading.
  • The integral is a running total. position += velocity is one.
  • Arc length lets you place things at even distances along any path.
