Maths 4

Differential Equations

An ordinary formula tells you where something is. A differential equation tells you only how it is changing, and leaves you to find out where that leads. Almost everything that moves, grows, flows or oscillates is described this way.

What a differential equation is

A differential equation is a rule for the rate of change of a quantity, written in terms of its current state. For example:

rate of change of position = velocity x′ = v

rate of change of velocity = force ÷ mass v′ = F / m

Those two lines are Newton's laws of motion, and they are differential equations. They do not say where the particle will be. They say how to get from now to a moment from now.

That makes them a natural fit for a computer. To solve a differential equation numerically you do exactly what it says, over and over, in small time steps:

repeat:
  work out the rate of change from the current state
  state += rate of change × dt

This is Euler's method. You met it in Lesson 5 as velocity += acceleration; position += velocity, and Maths 3 showed that it is a numerical integral. Every simulation in this course is that loop with a different rule for the rate of change.

The artistic appeal is that you design the law, not the shape. You never draw a spiral or an orbit; you write a rule and discover what it traces.

The spring: where oscillation comes from

A spring pulls back toward its rest position with a force proportional to how far it has been stretched. Add friction proportional to speed:

acceleration = −k · position − c · velocity

From that one line come sine waves (when c is zero), decaying wobbles (small c) and a smooth settle with no overshoot (large c). It is the most reused equation in motion design: anything that should bounce, wobble, follow with a lag or settle into place is a spring.

The example also shows that how you take the step matters. The two methods differ only in the order of two lines, but the first one steadily invents energy that is not there.

Choosing a method. Always update velocity before position (semi-implicit Euler): it costs nothing and is far more stable. Keep the time step small; if a simulation explodes, halve dt first. For higher accuracy there are better steppers, of which Runge–Kutta 4 (RK4) is the standard, and Verlet integration is the favourite for cloth and ropes. Try dt = 0.05 above to see even the good method start to struggle.

Phase space: drawing the state, not the object

A pendulum obeys almost the same equation as the spring, with a sine in it:

angular acceleration = −sin(angle) − c · speed

Its complete state at any moment is two numbers: its angle and its speed. Plot one against the other and you have a point in phase space. As time passes that point traces a path, and the paths from many different starting states form the phase portrait of the system: a picture of everything the pendulum can ever do.

Phase portraits are a rich source of imagery, because the differential equation defines a flow field (Lesson 5) with real structure: here, whirlpools where the pendulum comes to rest hanging down, and saddle points between them where it balances upright.

Orbits

Gravity pulls toward a centre with a strength that falls off with distance:

acceleration = −strength / distancep, toward the centre

With the real-world value p = 2, the orbit is a closed ellipse that retraces itself for ever. That closure is a special property of the inverse-square law. Change the exponent slightly and the ellipse no longer closes: it precesses, rotating a little each lap, and draws a rosette. So the exponent in a force law is a design parameter, and "wrong" physics is a spirograph.

Chaos: the Lorenz system

In 1963 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz reduced a model of air convection to three linked equations in three variables:

x′ = σ(y − x) y′ = x(ρ − z) − y z′ = xy − βz

The solution never settles down and never repeats, yet it stays on a definite butterfly-shaped structure called a strange attractor. It is also extremely sensitive to where it starts. The example traces two paths that begin one ten-thousandth of a unit apart. For a while they are indistinguishable, then they part company completely. That is chaos: fully deterministic, with no randomness anywhere, and unpredictable in practice.

Iterated maps: attractors without time steps

A close relative of the differential equation is the iterated map: a formula that takes a point and returns the next point, applied over and over. There is no dt; each application is a jump. Clifford Pickover's attractor uses nothing but sines and cosines:

x′ = sin(a·y) + c·cos(a·x) y′ = sin(b·x) + d·cos(b·y)

The individual points hop about apparently at random, but two million of them accumulate into smooth, folded, smoke-like forms. Four numbers define the whole image.

The rendering uses two ideas from earlier pages. Instead of drawing dots, it counts how many times each pixel is hit, building a density map. And because a few pixels are hit thousands of times while most are hit only a few, it takes the logarithm of the count (Maths 1) so that faint structure stays visible.

Partial differential equations: change across space

So far each equation tracked a few numbers through time. A partial differential equation (PDE) tracks a whole field, a value at every point of a grid, where each point's rate of change depends on its neighbours. The neighbour comparison is nearly always the Laplacian, written ∇²: how much a cell differs from the average of the cells around it.

EquationRuleWhat it does
Diffusion (heat)u′ = D·∇²uEach cell drifts toward the average of its neighbours. Blurs, spreads, smooths.
Waveu″ = c²·∇²uEach cell accelerates toward that average. It overshoots, so disturbances travel as ripples.
Reaction–diffusionTwo diffusion equations plus a reactionSpots, stripes and mazes (Lesson 8).

Notice the single difference between the first two: one prime or two. Diffusion sets the rate of change, the wave equation sets the acceleration. That is the entire difference between ink spreading in water and a ripple crossing a pond.

The wave equation needs the current grid and the previous one, because acceleration is a change in the rate of change. The picture is then lit using the gradient from Maths 3.

Key ideas

  • A differential equation gives the rate of change. Solving it numerically means stepping: state += rate × dt.
  • Update velocity before position, and keep the step small.
  • The damped spring is the universal source of oscillation and settling.
  • Phase space plots a system's state. Its portrait is a flow field with whirlpools and saddles.
  • Chaotic systems are deterministic but unpredictable, and trace strange attractors.
  • A PDE applies a rule to a whole grid using each cell's neighbours. Diffusion smooths, the wave equation ripples.

Practice

Three exercises

Do these in the maths sandbox file, or by editing the examples above.

  • Springy followers. Make a chain of twenty points in which each is pulled by a damped spring toward the one before it, and the first follows the mouse. Draw the chain as a ribbon using normals from Maths 3.
  • Invent a system. Write your own pair of equations, x′ = f(x, y) and y′ = g(x, y), using sines, products and constants. Draw its phase portrait from 300 random starts. Adjust until it has at least one whirlpool.
  • Attractor poster. Find four Clifford parameters of your own by trial, render at 1000 × 1000 with ten million points, and design a colour ramp for it.
