Maths 5

Waves, Curves, Chaos and Chance

Five more ideas from across mathematics that each open up a family of designs: building any shape from circles, smooth curves from a handful of points, order turning into chaos, probability as a way of shading, and sequences that spread themselves evenly.

Fourier series: everything is a sum of waves

In the early 1800s Joseph Fourier showed that any repeating signal, however angular, can be built by adding together plain sine waves of different frequencies and sizes. A square wave, for instance, is the sum of the odd harmonics, each one weaker in proportion to its frequency:

square(t) = sin(t) + sin(3t)/3 + sin(5t)/5 + sin(7t)/7 + …

There is a geometric way to see a sum of sines. Each sine is the height of a point going round a circle (Lesson 4). Adding sines means putting the centre of each circle on the rim of the one before. Such a chain of rotating circles is a set of epicycles, and the end of the chain traces the sum.

Change terms to 1 and you get a pure sine. Each added circle squares the wave off a little more. The idea runs the other way too: a Fourier transform takes any signal apart into its component waves, which is how music visualisers find the bass and treble, and how a set of epicycles can be computed that redraws any outline you give it.

For design, the lesson is that complex periodic motion is a sum of simple rotations. Lesson 4's spirograph is two terms. Lesson 3's fBm noise is the same principle with noise in place of sine: detail built by adding layers at rising frequency and falling strength.

Bézier curves: interpolation of interpolations

How do you get a smooth curve from a few points? A cubic Bézier curve, the kind in every vector drawing program and font, uses four control points and nothing but lerp, applied three levels deep. This is de Casteljau's algorithm:

  1. Lerp between each neighbouring pair of the four control points: three new points.
  2. Lerp between each neighbouring pair of those: two points.
  3. Lerp between those two: one point, which is on the curve.

Do that for every t from 0 to 1 and the final point sweeps out the curve. It starts at the first control point, ends at the last, and is pulled toward the middle two without touching them.

In practice you call ctx.bezierCurveTo and the browser does this for you. The reason to know the construction is that it explains how to control the curve: the line from an end point to its neighbouring control point is the tangent there (Maths 3). To join curves smoothly, keep those tangent lines in line across the join. A spline is a chain of such pieces. The Catmull–Rom spline chooses the control points automatically so that the curve passes through a list of points, which is the easy way to turn a random walk or a list of noise samples into a flowing line.

Chaos: the logistic map

Maths 4 showed chaos in three linked differential equations. It needs far less. Take one number between 0 and 1 and repeatedly apply:

x → r · x · (1 − x)

This was proposed as a model of an animal population: growth (r·x) limited by crowding (1 − x). What happens in the long run depends entirely on r, and the dependence is astonishing. The diagram below has r along the bottom, and above each r it plots the values that x keeps visiting once it has settled down.

For small r the population settles to a single value. Then it splits to alternate between two, then four, then eight, the splits coming faster and faster, until around r = 3.57 it becomes chaotic. Inside the chaos there are windows of order, and inside those windows are small copies of the whole diagram: it is a fractal. The same period-doubling route to chaos appears in dripping taps, heart rhythms and electronic circuits.

As a design tool, chaos gives you a deterministic source of variety with a single dial running from regular to wild. Use a chaotic map to drive a parameter and you can place a design precisely on the border between pattern and noise, which is where most interesting images live.

Probability as density

Lesson 2 used probability to choose things. It can also draw. The idea: scatter random candidate points, and keep each one with a probability equal to how dark the picture should be at that spot. Where the probability is high, many points survive and the area reads as dark. Tone is represented by density. This is rejection sampling, and it is how stippled drawings, halftones and grainy textures are made.

The darkness function below is a sphere lit from the upper left, computed with the dot product from Maths 2. Any function of position would do: noise, a distance field, the brightness of a photograph.

Try it: change the exponent 1.6. It is a shaping function (Maths 1) acting as a contrast control. Replace darkness with a noise function for stippled clouds.

Two more facts about probability earn their keep. The law of large numbers says averages of many random values are steady even though each value is not, which is why a stipple of 90,000 random dots looks smooth and why Monte Carlo rendering works. The central limit theorem says sums of many random values form a bell curve whatever the individual values were, which is why adding a few R() calls together gives a natural-looking spread.

Sequences that spread out

Random points clump. Lesson 7 fixed that with Poisson-disc sampling, which takes a fair amount of code. Number theory offers a one-line alternative. Take a suitable irrational number, step along by it repeatedly, and keep only the fractional part:

xi = fract(i · α)

If α is "very irrational" (hard to approximate by a fraction), each new point lands in the largest gap left by the previous ones. The best choice in one dimension is the reciprocal of the golden ratio, which is why the golden angle gave such even packing in Lesson 4's sunflower. In two dimensions the equivalent constant comes from the plastic number, the solution of x³ = x + 1. Such low-discrepancy sequences give evenly spread points with no neighbour checks and no stored state: point number i is computed directly.

Key ideas

  • Any periodic shape is a sum of sine waves, which is a chain of rotating circles.
  • A Bézier curve is three levels of lerp. Its end tangents point at the neighbouring control points.
  • The logistic map shows a simple rule passing from order through period doubling into chaos.
  • Keeping random points with a probability equal to darkness turns chance into shading.
  • Stepping by a well-chosen irrational number spreads points evenly with one line of code.

Three exercises

Do these in the maths sandbox file, or by editing the examples above.

  • Epicycle drawing. Give each circle in M5.1 a random radius and a random whole-number speed, positive or negative, and trace the end point in two dimensions instead of plotting its height. Whole-number speeds guarantee the figure closes.
  • Flowing line. Generate eight random points and join them with Bézier segments whose tangents line up at every join, so that the whole path is smooth.
  • Stippled terrain. Combine three pages: take the lit noise landscape from Maths 3 and render it as a stipple, using its shade as the keep-probability and a low-discrepancy sequence for the candidate points.
