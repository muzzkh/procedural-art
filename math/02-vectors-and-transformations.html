Maths 2

Vectors, Matrices and Complex Numbers

Maths 1 was about single numbers. Pictures live in two dimensions, so this page is about numbers that travel in pairs: how to measure them, compare them, and transform a whole plane of them at once.

Vectors

A vector is a pair (x, y) read as an arrow: a direction and a length. Four operations cover almost everything.

OperationFormulaMeaning
Add(ax + bx, ay + by)Follow one arrow, then the other. Position plus velocity.
Scale(ax * s, ay * s)Same direction, different length.
LengthMath.hypot(ax, ay)Pythagoras: √(x² + y²).
Normalise(ax / length, ay / length)Keep the direction, set the length to 1. A pure direction.

Subtracting two positions gives the arrow from one to the other, which is how you find "the direction toward the mouse". One more trick is worth memorising: swapping the components and negating one, (-y, x), turns any vector by exactly a quarter turn. That perpendicular is how you offset a line sideways or make a flow circulate instead of converge.

The dot product

a · b = ax·bx + ay·by

The dot product of two vectors is one number. For two directions of length 1 it measures how much they agree:

  • 1 when they point the same way,
  • 0 when they are at right angles,
  • −1 when they point in opposite ways.

(It equals the cosine of the angle between them, without your having to compute an angle.) This single line is the heart of all computer lighting: a surface is bright in proportion to how directly it faces the light. It is also how Perlin noise works internally, and how you ask "is this thing in front of me or behind me?"

Matrices: transforming the whole plane

A 2 × 2 matrix is four numbers that describe a transformation of every point at once. Applying it is two lines:

x′ = a·x + b·y y′ = c·x + d·y

Different choices of the four numbers give every transformation that keeps straight lines straight and the origin fixed:

Transformationa, b, c, d
Scalesx, 0, 0, sy
Rotate by angle θcos θ, −sin θ, sin θ, cos θ
Shear (slant)1, k, 0, 1
Mirror left to right−1, 0, 0, 1

Two transformations can be combined into one by multiplying their matrices. The order matters: rotating then shearing is not the same as shearing then rotating. When you call ctx.rotate and ctx.scale, the canvas is multiplying matrices on your behalf, and the fern in Lesson 6 is nothing but four of these matrices, each with a shift added, applied at random.

The red and yellow arrows show where the two unit directions land. That is a useful way to read any matrix: its first column is where "one step right" goes, and its second column is where "one step down" goes. Maths 6 takes matrices further: determinants, inverses, chains, 3D and eigenvectors.

Symmetry

A design is symmetric when a transformation leaves it looking the same. Mathematicians catalogue these as symmetry groups, and a designer can use the same catalogue directly: draw one small motif, then apply every transformation in the group to it.

  • Rotational symmetry of order n: rotate by 1/n of a turn, n times. Pinwheels, flowers.
  • Dihedral symmetry: the rotations plus a mirror. Snowflakes, mandalas, kaleidoscopes.
  • Wallpaper symmetry: add repetition by translation in two directions. There are exactly 17 distinct ways to do it. Islamic tilings and Escher's prints are the famous examples.

Complex numbers

A complex number is a point on the plane written as one number, z = x + y·i, with one special rule: i² = −1. That rule sounds arbitrary, but its effect is geometric and simple:

  • Adding complex numbers adds them as vectors: a shift.
  • Multiplying complex numbers adds their angles and multiplies their lengths: a rotation and a scaling in one step.

So complex arithmetic is a compact way to rotate and scale. Squaring a number doubles its angle and squares its length:

z² = (x² − y²) + (2xy)·i

Those are the two lines inside the Mandelbrot loop in Lesson 6. Repeating z → z² + c stretches, wraps and shifts the plane over and over, and the boundary between points that escape and points that stay is a fractal. In the Mandelbrot set c is the pixel. If instead you fix c and let the starting z be the pixel, you get a Julia set, a different one for every c. Here c travels slowly round a circle.

Try it: replace the two update lines with the formula for z³: real part x*x*x - 3*x*y*y, imaginary part 3*x*x*y - y*y*y. Cubing triples the angle, and the figure gains three-fold symmetry.

Key ideas

  • A vector is a direction with a length. Normalising keeps only the direction.
  • The dot product measures agreement between directions, from −1 to 1.
  • (-y, x) is a quarter turn.
  • A matrix transforms the whole plane. Multiplying matrices chains transformations, and order matters.
  • Symmetry is a set of transformations applied to one motif.
  • Multiplying complex numbers rotates and scales. Iterating that makes fractals.

Practice

Three exercises

Do these in the maths sandbox file, or by editing the examples above.

  • Lit tiles. Make a grid of squares, give each a random "facing" direction, and shade it by the dot product with a light direction. Then let the light direction rotate over time.
  • Wallpaper. Take the mandala motif from M2.3 and, instead of rotating about one centre, repeat a small symmetric unit across the whole canvas in a grid, mirroring every other copy.
  • Your own fern. Write three or four matrices with shifts, apply them at random 50,000 times as in Lesson 6, and adjust the numbers until the result looks like a plant that does not exist.
