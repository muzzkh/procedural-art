Web Design 1

Generative Effects for Websites

The most memorable websites of recent years share a habit: something on the page is alive. A hero image that reveals a second version of itself under the cursor, a background that drifts, a headline that leans toward the pointer. This lesson builds six of those effects from the techniques you already have.

What generative design is for on a website

On a web page the generative element is rarely the content. Its job is to give the page a character and to reward attention: the visitor moves, and the page answers. That sets three rules which art for its own sake does not have.

  • It must never get in the way. Text stays readable, buttons stay clickable, the page loads fast.
  • It must look good untouched. Many visitors never move the mouse over it, and phones have no hover at all.
  • It should express the brand. The palette, shapes and motion are chosen to say something about whose site it is.

Every effect below is shown on a canvas so that you can edit it. The next lesson puts them into real pages.

The dual-design reveal

This is the effect in which a hero shows one design, and moving the cursor over it uncovers a second, different design through a soft-edged hole that follows the pointer. It works because it turns a still image into a small discovery: the visitor finds out that there is something underneath, and keeps moving to see more. Sites use it to show two sides of one subject: a portrait and the same person in a helmet, a product and its insides, a building by day and by night.

The recipe has three parts:

  1. Two complete designs of the same composition, aligned so that key elements sit in the same place. The alignment is what makes the reveal feel like looking through one into the other.
  2. A mask that follows the cursor. Here it is a blob: a circle whose radius is wobbled by noise sampled round a ring (Lesson 3), so that its edge moves like liquid.
  3. Life in the mask. It trails behind the pointer through smoothing, and it swells when the pointer moves fast, so that the hole feels like something the visitor is dragging through the page.

Both designs here are generated from the same seed: the same flow of lines, drawn once quietly and once loudly.

Try it: change the smoothing from 0.12 to 0.03 for a heavy, lazy blob. Set the wobble multiplier to zero for a plain circle and see how much of the charm was in the edge. Replace either design with anything you built in the course.

A reveal that lingers

The blob shows the second design only where the cursor is now. A variant leaves a trail: where the pointer has been stays revealed for a moment and then heals over. It needs a third hidden canvas, the mask, which is treated like wet ink. Every frame it is faded slightly, and a soft spot is painted at the cursor. The second design is then drawn only where the mask has ink, using the canvas's compositing modes:

  • destination-out erases what is already there: used to fade the mask.
  • destination-in keeps what is there only where the new drawing is opaque: used to cut the second design to the mask's shape.

A background that notices you

The most common generative element on the web is a reactive background behind a hero section: a grid of dots or lines that bends away from the cursor and springs back. It is cheap to draw, readable under text, and works on any brand by changing two colours.

Each dot has a home position and a current position. The cursor pushes it away with a strength that falls off with distance (the bell curve of Maths 1), and a spring pulls it home (Maths 4). The dot's size grows with how far it has been displaced, so the disturbance is visible as a ripple.

Type that leans in

Headlines can respond too. Draw each letter separately, and let its position, rotation and weight depend on its distance from the cursor. Small displacements read as playfulness. Keep them small: a headline must remain a headline.

Living gradients

Soft, slowly shifting colour fields are now a standard background for technology and finance sites. They are made by drawing a few very large, very soft blobs of colour whose centres drift on noise, and letting them blend. A faint grain on top stops the result from looking like plastic and hides the banding that smooth gradients show on some screens.

Thumbnails that make themselves

Blogs, documentation sites and portfolios need an image for every article or project, and most have none. A generative thumbnail, seeded by the title, gives every item its own artwork in a consistent house style, with no design work per item. It is Special Topic 2's brand system applied to a page. Here the card under the cursor also comes to life, which is how such cards usually behave on hover.

Key ideas

  • On a website the generative element gives character and rewards attention. It must not obstruct.
  • A reveal needs two aligned designs and a mask that follows the cursor with some life of its own.
  • Compositing modes turn one canvas into a mask for another.
  • Springs and a bell-curve push make any grid reactive.
  • Every effect needs an idle state that looks good without any input.
  • Seeds give every article, card and user its own artwork in one house style.

Practice

Three exercises

  • Your own reveal. Replace both designs in W1.1 with two treatments of one of your course pieces: for instance a line drawing and its filled, coloured version. Make sure they align.
  • A different mask. Change the blob into a shape with meaning: a vertical wipe that follows the cursor's x position, a ring, or several smaller blobs that orbit the pointer.
  • One brand, three effects. Pick two colours and a typeface for an imaginary company. Restyle W1.3, W1.5 and W1.6 so that all three clearly belong to it.
