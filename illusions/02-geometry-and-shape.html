Illusions 2

Geometry and Shape

Straight lines that look bent, equal lengths that look unequal, and shapes with no outline that are plainly there. The geometric illusions are the oldest in the scientific literature and the easiest to generate. This lesson builds six of them with sliders for the variables the research identified, and introduces the method by which an illusion is measured.

Tilt: the café wall

Rows of black and white tiles are laid with every other row shifted sideways, with a thin line of mortar between rows. The mortar lines are parallel. They look like long wedges, alternately converging and diverging. Richard Gregory and Priscilla Heard studied the figure in "Border Locking and the Café Wall Illusion" (Perception, 1979), named after the tiled front of a café in Bristol, and established the conditions that this example lets you test:

  • The offset matters. With no shift the tiles form a plain chequerboard of columns and nothing tilts.
  • The mortar must be between the tiles in brightness. With mid-grey mortar the effect is strong. With mortar as dark as the dark tiles or as light as the light tiles it almost disappears.
  • The mortar must be thin.

The reason offered is local. At each place where a dark tile sits diagonally across the mortar from another dark tile, the grey mortar between them joins the two into a tiny slanted step. A whole row of small tilts, all the same way, is read as one long tilted line.

Bending: lines over a fan

Many nineteenth-century illusions share one structure: a straight line crossed by many other lines at an angle appears displaced or bent, as if the acute angles were being seen as larger than they are. In Ewald Hering's figure of 1861, two parallel lines over a fan of rays appear to bow outward. The Zöllner, Wundt and Poggendorff figures are relatives.

Fade the fan with the slider. The two red lines are drawn by the same two commands throughout.

Length, and how an illusion is measured

In the Müller-Lyer figure, published in 1889, a line that ends in outward-pointing fins looks longer than an equal line that ends in arrowheads. It is the most studied illusion there is. One influential account is Richard Gregory's, from "Distortion of Visual Space as Inappropriate Constancy Scaling" (Nature, 1963): the fins resemble the edges of a corner seen in perspective, the visual system treats one line as further away than the other, and it scales their apparent sizes accordingly. The account is still disputed, which is normal in this field.

How strong is the illusion? A demonstration that says "these are equal, believe it or not" cannot answer that. The standard technique can, and it is a small piece of interaction design called the method of adjustment:

  1. Show a fixed standard and an adjustable comparison.
  2. Ask the person to adjust the comparison until the two look equal.
  3. The size of their error is the size of the illusion, in real units.

Do it now. Drag the first slider until the lower line looks the same length as the upper one. Then reveal the answer. Most people set the lower line noticeably too short.

Now change the fin angle and repeat. Sharper fins give a larger error. A set of such measurements at several angles is a small scientific result, obtained with a slider and a ruler made of pixels. This lesson's challenge is to collect one properly.

Size: the company a shape keeps

In the Ebbinghaus figure a disc surrounded by large discs looks smaller than the same disc surrounded by small ones. Size, like brightness, is judged against the neighbours. The animated version is more convincing than the static one, because the eye is very sensitive to change: the orange disc below appears to swell and shrink as its companions do the opposite. Its radius is a constant in the code. Fade the companions to check.

Shapes with no edges

In 1955 Gaetano Kanizsa published figures in which a bright triangle is seen lying over three black discs, although the triangle has no outline and is exactly the colour of the paper. The discs each have a wedge missing, the missing wedges line up, and the visual system prefers the explanation "a shape is covering three discs" to "three odd discs happen to be aimed at each other". It then supplies the shape, complete with edges that seem faintly visible across the empty gaps, and often makes it look brighter than its surroundings.

This is a generator. Any polygon can be suggested by cutting its corners out of discs. The control is alignment: turn the discs and the shape is gone, though nothing has been erased.

Illusory contours are useful well beyond the demonstration. A logo, a poster or a pattern can imply a form by what it leaves out, and the viewer's own completion of it is more engaging than an outline would be. The limits are worth knowing: the inducing shapes must be clearly "incomplete" versions of something simple, and the gaps between them must not be too large compared with their size.

Direction: circles that look like a spiral

James Fraser's "A New Visual Illusion of Direction" (British Journal of Psychology, 1908) introduced what he called the twisted cord: a line made of short slanted strands, alternately dark and light, like a rope of two colours. Laid straight, such a cord appears tilted in the direction of its strands. Laid in concentric circles, each circle appears to run inward, and the whole reads as a spiral. People asked to trace one with a finger are surprised to arrive back where they started.

The strands' slant is the cause, so it is the slider. At zero the circles are plain dashed circles. The last control draws over one of them to show that it closes.

What these have in common

The café wall, the fan and the twisted cord are one idea at three scales. A long contour is assembled by the visual system from many small local measurements of direction. Bias every local measurement the same way, by a small slanted element or a crossing line, and the bias adds up along the whole contour. The same summing explains why the effects are stronger with many small elements than with a few large ones, and why they survive when you know the truth: the summing happens before anything you would call thinking.

Key ideas

  • The café wall needs shifted rows and thin mortar of a brightness between the tiles.
  • Lines crossed at an angle appear displaced. A fan of rays bends straight lines.
  • The method of adjustment turns an illusion into a number: the error a person makes when asked to match.
  • Size is judged relative to neighbours.
  • Aligned gaps are completed into shapes, with edges and brightness supplied by the viewer.
  • Small local biases in direction add up along a contour, turning circles into an apparent spiral.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing the "straight" line as several segments. If it is not one lineTo, a sceptic can fairly ask whether it is really straight.
  • Thick mortar, large strands, few rays. These effects come from many small elements.
  • Testing only on yourself. You know the answer, and knowing weakens some of these. Ask someone who has not seen the code.

Illusions challenge 2

Measure Your Own Eyes

Turn one geometric illusion into a small experiment. Collect real measurements from yourself and one other person, and draw the result as a chart.

Requirements

  • One illusion from this lesson, with one variable that you set at five levels: fin angle, number of rays, row shift, companion size, or strand slant.
  • A matching task for the viewer by the method of adjustment: they adjust something until two things look equal, or until a line looks straight or level.
  • Twenty trials for each person: four at each level, in a shuffled order from a seeded generator, each starting from a different random setting.
  • The viewer never sees the true values during a run.
  • At the end, a chart of the average error at each level, with every individual trial shown as a dot.
  • One level is a control at which the theory predicts no illusion. Report the error there too.

Hints

  • Use the arrow keys for adjustment and a key to confirm. A slider's own position gives the answer away.
  • Start each trial sometimes too large and sometimes too small, or people simply stop at the first plausible setting.
  • Record every trial as a line of text: person, level, starting value, final value. Lesson 10's habit of saving the recipe applies to data as well.

Stretch goals

  • Add a second illusion and test whether people who are strongly fooled by one are strongly fooled by the other.
  • Export the trials as a CSV file.

You are done when

  • Your chart shows the error changing with your variable, and close to zero at the control level.
  • You can state the size of the illusion as a number with a unit.
