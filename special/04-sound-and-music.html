Special Topic 4

Sound and Generative Music

Sound is made by rules as readily as pictures are. The browser has a complete synthesiser built in, and every idea in this course, seeds, weighted choice, noise, automata, physics, makes music when it is pointed at pitch and time instead of position and colour.

About the examples. Each one is silent until you click its canvas, and plays quietly. Browsers do not allow a page to make sound before the visitor has interacted with it, so starting on a click is required as well as polite. Press Run again on an example to stop it.

How sound is made in the browser

The Web Audio API works by connecting small processing units, called nodes, into a chain that ends at the speakers. Three are enough to make music:

Oscillator
Produces a steady tone at a frequency you set. Its wave shape sets the character: a sine is pure, a triangle is soft, a sawtooth is bright and buzzy, a square is hollow.
Gain
A volume control. Changing it over time gives a note its shape.
Destination
The speakers.

A raw oscillator switched on and off clicks unpleasantly. A note needs an envelope: the volume rises quickly (the attack) and then dies away (the decay). A fast attack and long decay sounds plucked or struck. A slow attack sounds bowed or blown.

Pitch

Pitch is frequency, in cycles per second. Musical pitch is exponential, like the growth in Maths 1: each octave doubles the frequency, and each of the twelve semitones in an octave multiplies it by the twelfth root of two. Numbering the notes (middle C is 60, the A above it is 69 and sounds at 440) gives:

frequency = 440 × 2(note − 69) / 12

Choosing notes at random from all twelve semitones sounds like a cat on a piano. Restricting them to a scale, a subset of the twelve, makes almost anything sound intentional. The five-note pentatonic scale is the most forgiving: no two of its notes clash.

A melody from a rule

The simplest generative melody is a random walk (Lesson 2) along a scale: each note is the previous one, moved up or down by a small weighted step. Small steps are common and leaps are rare, as in sung melodies. Note lengths come from a weighted choice too.

Timing matters. Sound must be scheduled precisely, and the animation loop is not precise. So the code looks a little way ahead and books each note in advance on the audio clock, audio.currentTime, which is exact.

Rhythm from a rule

Rhythm is a pattern of hits and rests on a grid of steps. A remarkably musical way to generate one is to spread a number of hits as evenly as possible over a number of steps. Three hits over eight steps gives the pattern heard across Cuban, West African and rock music. Five over eight, seven over sixteen and others are equally familiar. Godfried Toussaint described these as Euclidean rhythms in 2005.

One line decides whether step i is a hit:

const isHit = (i, hits, steps) => (i * hits) % steps < hits;

Layering several such patterns with different numbers, each on its own sound, gives a groove. The drums are synthesised as well: a kick is a sine wave whose pitch drops quickly, and a hi-hat is a very short burst of noise.

One system, heard and seen

The most satisfying audio-visual work does not add sound to a picture or a picture to sound. A single system produces events, and each event is both drawn and played. Because they have one cause, the eye and ear agree completely.

Any simulation from this course generates events: a collision, a cell coming alive, a branch splitting, a particle crossing a line. Here balls bounce inside a circle. Each bounce is one event. It plays a note whose pitch comes from the ball's size, and it draws a ripple where the ball struck. Small balls are high and frequent, large balls low and slow, and the music is whatever the physics happens to do.

The lesson of Lesson 2 holds here too: structure with a measured amount of chance. Fix the scale, the tempo and the form, and let randomness choose within them. Brian Eno, who coined the term generative music in the 1990s, built pieces from a few loops of unequal length that never line up the same way twice.

Key ideas

  • Sound is a chain of nodes: oscillator, gain, destination.
  • A note needs an envelope. Its shape is the instrument's character.
  • Pitch is exponential. Restrict notes to a scale and randomness sounds deliberate.
  • Schedule sounds ahead of time on the audio clock, not in the animation loop.
  • Spreading hits evenly over steps gives rhythms found all over the world.
  • Let one system emit events, and both draw and play each one.

Special challenge 4

A Piece for Eyes and Ears

Build a system that generates music and image together, from one seed, in which every sound has a visible cause.

Requirements

  • Silent until clicked, and quiet: a master volume no higher than 0.3.
  • One simulation or rule system from the course produces events. Each event both makes a sound and leaves a mark.
  • At least two voices with different envelopes and wave shapes.
  • Pitches come from a scale, and rhythm has a pulse: events are aligned to a grid of steps, or the system itself is periodic.
  • Slow change over a minute or more, driven by noise, so that it does not simply repeat.
  • Seeded: the same seed plays the same piece.
  • Keys to stop, restart and change seed.

Hints

  • Get the simulation running and drawing first, with a console.log where each event happens. Then replace the log with a sound.
  • Fewer notes sound better. If it is a blur, raise the threshold for what counts as an event.
  • Low notes should be long and rare, high notes short and frequent.

Stretch goals

  • Add a filter or an echo, with its settings driven by the picture.
  • Record it: connect the master gain to a MediaStreamDestination and use a MediaRecorder, together with the canvas stream from Lesson 15.

You are done when

  • With your eyes shut it sounds like music, and with the sound off it looks like a piece.
  • Someone watching can tell which thing on screen made which sound.
