Special Topic 2

Practical Uses

Procedural methods are not only for gallery walls. They are a working tool in games, film, graphic design, product design, data visualisation and software. This page surveys where they are used, why, and what the generators look like.

When a procedure beats a hand

Making things by hand is often faster for one item. A procedure earns its cost when one of these is true:

NeedWhy a procedure helpsExample
ScaleMore content is needed than anyone could makeA planet of terrain, a forest of distinct trees
VariationMany items that are related but not identicalLabels for 60 products, 10,000 avatars
PersonalisationOne unique item per personA poster from your birth date, a ring from your voice
ResponsivenessThe result must follow changing inputA chart that redraws from live data, a logo that reacts to sound
ExplorationYou want to see a thousand options before choosingFacade patterns, textile designs
Late changesThe brief will change, and redoing it by hand is costly"Make all the windows 10% wider"
Small sizeA formula is smaller than its outputA texture of a few lines of code instead of megabytes of image
ConstraintsRules must hold in every instanceMinimum wall thickness, brand colours, legal margins

If none applies, draw it by hand. Recognising that is part of the skill.

Games and film

Entertainment is the largest employer of procedural techniques. In games the content is generated while the game runs, or by tools the level designers use. In film it is generated offline, most often in Houdini, where every effect is a network of procedures that can be rerun when the director changes a shot.

  • Worlds: terrain, erosion, rivers, biomes, cities, road networks, building interiors.
  • Living things: forests, grass, fur and hair, crowds of thousands with varied clothing and motion.
  • Effects: fire, smoke, water, explosions, destruction, magic. Nearly all of visual effects is simulation (Lesson 8, Maths 4).
  • Surfaces: materials built from noise instead of photographs, covered below.
  • Structure: levels, puzzles, loot, quests and names.

A level generator is a good first practical project because its requirements are strict: every room must be reachable, nothing may overlap, and it must work for every seed. The classic method combines two ideas you know. Recursive subdivision (Lesson 7) divides the map into regions. Each region gets a room. Then, as the recursion returns, the two halves of every split are joined by a corridor, which guarantees that the whole map is connected.

Graphic design and brand identity

A traditional brand identity is a fixed logo and a style guide. A generative identity is a system: rules that produce an unlimited family of marks, patterns and layouts that all clearly belong together. Since the 2000s, cultural institutions, festivals, universities and technology companies have used such systems so that every poster, ticket or business card is unique yet on-brand.

The design principle is to separate two kinds of rule:

  • Brand rules are fixed for every instance: layout, typeface, line weight, the kind of motif.
  • Instance rules vary, and come from a seed: colour, counts, wave shapes.

Too many fixed rules and the outputs are monotonous. Too few and they do not look related. Below, each product name seeds its own label. Edit the list of names: a new product gets a matching label for free, which is precisely the commercial value.

Automated layout

Layout itself can be generated. A poster system takes content (a headline, a date) and a seed, and produces compositions: a grid from recursive subdivision, the headline fitted to the largest suitable block, the remaining blocks filled by weighted choice from a small vocabulary. Designers use tools like this to produce a campaign of fifty related posters, or to explore options before refining one by hand. Type a headline and press the button.

Textures and materials

In 3D graphics, film and games, most surfaces are not photographs. They are procedural materials: formulas, mostly built on noise, that compute the colour, roughness and bumpiness of a surface at any point. Their advantages are practical. They have no resolution limit, they never visibly repeat, they need almost no storage, and one parameter can turn new oak into weathered oak. Whole applications exist for authoring them as networks of nodes.

Each quadrant below is one short formula. All four are applications of Lesson 3 and Maths 1. The Textures section takes this subject much further: structure, cell noise, weathering, and the maps a renderer needs.

  • Wood: rings are the distance from a centre, wobbled by noise, wrapped by fract.
  • Marble: a sine wave across the surface, with its input pushed around by fBm noise.
  • Brushed metal: noise sampled with a very high frequency in one direction and a very low one in the other.
  • Woven cloth: two sets of stripes, with a checker rule deciding which is on top.

Data as the input

When the numbers driving a generative system are measurements, the result is data visualisation if the aim is to read values accurately, and data art if the aim is to give the data a character. The two share all their techniques. The discipline that separates good data-driven work from decoration is a clear division of labour: the data decides the large-scale form, and the procedure adds only texture. If noise is strong enough to change what the data says, the piece has stopped being about the data.

Below, twelve monthly values shape the outline of every ring. Noise adds a hand-drawn wobble that is deliberately much smaller than the differences in the data. Replace the numbers with your own: rainfall, steps walked, hours slept.

Interfaces: default avatars

Every service with user accounts needs a picture for people who have not uploaded one. Generating it from the username gives each person something recognisable and costs nothing to store, because it can be recomputed at any time. The requirements are stricter than they look: the images must be told apart at a glance, must survive being shown at 16 pixels, and must never accidentally form anything offensive, which is one reason abstract symmetric grids are the usual choice.

Architecture: a facade that follows the sun

Buildings use perforated and louvred screens to cut glare and heat while keeping daylight and views. On a curved facade each panel faces a slightly different way, so each needs a different amount of shading. Drawing hundreds of unique panels by hand is not practical. Computing them is trivial: for each panel, measure how directly the sun strikes it (a dot product, Maths 2) and close it in proportion. Move the sun and the whole facade re-derives itself.

Textiles and surface pattern: the seamless repeat

Fabric, wallpaper, wrapping paper and game textures are all made from one small tile repeated edge to edge. The tile must join itself invisibly. The procedural solution is simple: whenever a motif is drawn near an edge, draw it again shifted by one tile width or height, so that the part that leaves on one side re-enters on the other. The example builds a tile that way on a hidden canvas and then repeats it across the fabric. The outlined square shows a single tile.

Maps, and the names on them

Fantasy maps for novels, tabletop games and video games are a small industry, and most are generated. The map below combines three techniques from the course: an island from noise with a radial falloff (Lesson 3), territories from nearest-seed regions (Voronoi, Lesson 7), and, new here, names from a grammar. A name generator is a tiny procedural system of its own: lists of syllables and endings, chosen by weighted chance. The same idea, scaled up, writes item descriptions, quests and dialogue in games.

Machine learning: synthetic training data

A model that finds objects in images needs thousands of examples in which every object is already outlined and named. Labelling photographs by hand is slow and expensive. A procedural generator produces the picture and its labels together, because it knows exactly what it drew and where. The generator varies everything that should not matter, such as background, lighting, position, size and clutter, so that the model learns only what does. This is called domain randomisation, and it is widely used in robotics and industrial inspection.

What you deliver

Practical work ends in one of four kinds of deliverable, and it is worth agreeing which before you start.

An output
One image, video or object. The code is your private tool, like a brush. The client never sees it.
A set of assets
Hundreds of generated items: icons, textures, labels, levels. You curate, the client receives files.
A tool
The generator itself, with an interface, for other people to use. This needs sensible parameter ranges, guard rails and documentation, and is far more work than the algorithm.
A live system
Code that runs for the audience: on a website, in a game, in an installation. It must be fast, robust, and correct for every input it will ever see.

Special Topic 10 returns to these fields with the research literature: how to measure a generator, how to search for outputs you can recognise but not construct, and how cities, terrain and engineering parts are generated in practice.

Each step down that list raises the standard. A private tool only has to work for the seeds you try. A live system has to work for all of them, which is the lesson of the contact sheet in Lesson 10.

Working practices that matter in practice

  • Make it reproducible. Record the seed and the code version with every output you deliver. A client will ask for "that one again, but bigger" six months later.
  • Separate content from style. Text, data and brand colours go in as inputs. They will change.
  • Encode the constraints. Margins, minimum sizes and contrast rules belong in the code, not in your memory.
  • Test in bulk. Generate five hundred and look for the worst one.
  • Leave room for a hand. The best results usually come from generating many options and then choosing or adjusting. A procedure is a collaborator, not a replacement for judgement.
  • Know the rights. Be clear about who owns the code and who owns the outputs, and what licences cover any fonts, images or data you feed in.

Key ideas

  • Use a procedure when you need scale, variation, personalisation, responsiveness, exploration or guaranteed constraints.
  • A level generator must be correct for every seed. Building connection into the algorithm is better than checking for it afterwards.
  • A generative identity separates fixed brand rules from seeded instance rules.
  • Procedural materials compute a surface from formulas, with no resolution limit and no repeats.
  • In data-driven work, the data sets the form and the procedure adds only texture.
  • Decide whether you are delivering an output, assets, a tool or a live system. Each demands more robustness than the last.

Special challenge 2

A Generator Someone Could Use

Pick a real, practical job and build the generator for it. The test is not whether it is beautiful but whether a person with that job could use the results tomorrow.

Choose one brief

  • Product range. A label or packaging system for a product line of at least eight items, seeded by product name, with space for real text.
  • Level generator. Levels for a simple game: guaranteed path from start to exit, with keys, doors or hazards placed by rule, and difficulty controlled by one number.
  • Avatar system. Default profile pictures for an app: readable at 32 pixels, clearly distinct from one another, never accidentally offensive or confusable.
  • Data portrait. A real dataset of your own (a year of weather, sleep, spending or music listening) turned into a printable image that stays faithful to the numbers.

Requirements

  • A written brief in a comment at the top: who it is for, what they need, what counts as a failure.
  • A clear split between fixed rules and seeded rules, labelled in the code.
  • At least three constraints that hold in every output, enforced by code. Examples: text never overlaps the pattern, every room is reachable, contrast is always sufficient.
  • A contact sheet of at least 24 outputs.
  • An export in the format the job needs: PNG at the right size, SVG, or data as text.
  • Every output is labelled with or traceable to its seed.

Hints

  • Write the failure cases first. "The exit is unreachable" and "two labels look identical" are requirements in disguise.
  • Where a constraint is hard to guarantee by construction, generate, test, and regenerate with the next seed until it passes.
  • Show your contact sheet to someone and ask which output is the worst. Then fix the rule that produced it.

You are done when

  • You have generated 200 outputs and checked that none breaks a constraint.
  • Someone else can produce a new output without reading the code.
