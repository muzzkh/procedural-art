Special Topic 8

Publishing

Work that stays in a folder teaches only its maker. This page covers getting it seen and, if you wish, sold: a portfolio, documentation, licences, prints and editions. It is practical guidance, not legal or financial advice.

A portfolio

A portfolio is an edit, not an archive. Its job is to let a stranger understand in a minute what you make and whether they want more of it.

  • Show less. Eight to fifteen strong pieces. One weak piece lowers the whole, because viewers assume you cannot tell the difference.
  • Show series. Three to six outputs from one system say more than one, and demonstrate that the system is good, not lucky.
  • Lead with the best. Most visitors see only the first screen.
  • Give each piece a title, a year and a line. One sentence on what it is and how it was made. Not a tutorial.
  • Show it in the world. A photograph of the framed print, the plot on the machine, the installation in its room. Generative work is often dismissed as "just an image". Evidence of the object answers that.
  • Make it fast. Large images, properly compressed. A live sketch or two is an asset, but the page must not depend on them.
  • Say who you are and how to reach you.

Your own website, however plain, is worth having. Platforms change their rules and disappear. Use them to be found, and point them home.

Documentation

For generative work the record of how is part of the work, and you will not remember it. For every finished piece keep:

Licences

By default, in most countries, you hold the copyright in what you make, and nobody else may copy or reuse it without permission. A licence is permission granted in advance, on stated terms. You do not have to grant any. If you want people to be able to share or build on your work, standard licences save everyone from guessing.

Generative work raises a question that ordinary images do not: the code and the outputs are separate things, and can be licensed separately. A common arrangement is to publish the code openly, so that others can learn from it, while keeping the outputs of your own seeds as your artwork. State both clearly.

The other side of this is what you use. Fonts, photographs, palettes sampled from someone's work, code from tutorials and libraries all come with terms. Check them before you sell anything that includes them. Fonts in particular often permit use in images but not redistribution.

Prints and editions

Special Topic 3 covered making a print. Selling one adds a few conventions.

Open edition
Printed as long as people want it. Lower price, no scarcity.
Limited edition
A fixed number, declared in advance and never exceeded. Each print is numbered, as in 7/25, and usually signed.
Unique outputs
The generative option: each print in a series comes from its own seed, so every buyer has a different piece from the same system. The series size is fixed. The example above works this way.
Artist's proofs
A few extra prints outside the numbered edition, kept by the artist. Marked A/P. Keep these few, and say how many exist.
Certificate of authenticity
A signed document stating the title, edition and details. Useful for anything sold at a meaningful price.
  • Honour the edition. Do not reprint a sold-out edition in another size or colour. An edition is a promise.
  • Print quality. For work meant to last, use pigment inks on acid-free paper, and say so. Order a proof before selling.
  • Pricing. Start from your costs: printing, packaging, postage, any platform's commission, and your time. Look at what comparable work by people at your stage sells for. It is easier to raise prices later than to lower them.
  • Packaging. Prints are damaged in the post more often than anywhere else. Flat, between rigid boards, or rolled in a strong tube.
  • The practical side. Selling work is income, and may have tax and consumer-law consequences that depend on where you live. Find out the rules that apply to you.

Sharing the process

People who follow generative art are unusually interested in how it is made. Process is material you already have:

  • The contact sheet of a hundred outputs beside the one you chose.
  • The bug that turned out better than the plan.
  • A short video of the plotter at work, or the simulation growing.
  • The same seed at three stages of the system's development.

Write about your work in plain language, in the first person, about what you were trying to do. Avoid describing the algorithm as though it were the point.

Key ideas

  • A portfolio is a strict edit: few pieces, shown as series, with evidence of the physical work.
  • Record the seed, parameters and code version of everything you finish.
  • Code and outputs can be licensed separately. Say which terms apply to each.
  • Check the terms of everything you build on: fonts, images, libraries.
  • An edition is a promise. Unique-output series are the natural form for generative prints.
  • Your process is worth showing.

Special challenge 8

Put One Series into the World

Take a series you have made, such as the ten pieces from Special Topic 7, and publish it properly: documented, licensed, and presented on a page of its own.

Requirements

  • A portfolio page for the series: a title, a statement of two or three sentences, between six and ten outputs, each with its own title or number.
  • The page is fast and plain: static images, readable on a phone, no dependence on scripts to show the work.
  • A record for every piece: seed, parameters, code version, date and dimensions, in a file kept with the work.
  • A stated licence for the images, and a separate one for the code, with a sentence on why you chose each.
  • A decision on editions: open, limited or unique outputs, the size, and how prints would be numbered and signed.
  • A generated label or certificate for each piece, drawing its details from the record.
  • One piece of process material: a contact sheet, a short write-up, or a video.
  • A list of every outside ingredient (fonts, libraries, data) and its licence.

Hints

  • Write the statement before choosing the images. It tells you which belong.
  • Export images at about 2000 pixels on the long side and compress them. Test the page on a phone over a slow connection.

You are done when

  • A stranger could understand the series in one minute.
  • You could regenerate any piece from your records alone.
