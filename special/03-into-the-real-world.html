Special Topic 3

Into the Real World

A screen forgives everything. Paper, plywood, steel and plastic do not. This page is about turning procedural designs into physical things: prints for a wall, drawings from a pen plotter, parts from a laser cutter or a mill, and objects from a 3D printer.

What changes when it becomes physical

On screenIn the world
Units are pixelsUnits are millimetres. Everything must have a real size.
A line can be any width, even zeroA line has the width of the pen, the laser beam or the cutter
Shapes can overlap and floatParts must hold together. Cut out a ring and its centre falls out.
Millions of colours, glowingThe inks, threads or materials you actually have, lit by the room
Detail is freeDetail costs machine time, and below a certain size it breaks or vanishes
UndoMaterial, and sometimes a tool, is used up

The good news is that procedural design is unusually well suited to fabrication. Machines want exact geometry, and that is what your code already produces. And physical limits are just more constraints: a minimum line spacing or wall thickness goes into the rules like a margin does.

The rule that follows from this table: design in real units from the start. Decide that your drawing is 200 mm wide, write every size in millimetres, and convert to pixels only for the on-screen preview. Lesson 10's unit-square method is the same idea.

Prints, posters and wall art

Resolution

A print viewed at arm's length needs about 300 pixels per inch (dpi). Large pieces seen from further away can drop to 150. The arithmetic is short:

pixels = millimetres ÷ 25.4 × dpi

An A2 poster (420 × 594 mm) at 300 dpi is 4961 × 7016 pixels. This is why resolution independence matters: a sketch written in fixed pixels cannot be printed large. Browsers also limit canvas size, so very large prints are rendered as several tiles and joined, or exported as vector files instead.

Colour and paper

  • Screens mix light (red, green, blue). Printers mix ink (cyan, magenta, yellow, black). Bright saturated screen colours, especially greens, blues and oranges, cannot all be printed. Ask for a proof before a large run.
  • Leave a bleed: extend the artwork about 3 mm past the trim line on every side if it should run to the edge, and keep anything important at least 5 mm inside.
  • For wall art, giclée (archival inkjet on cotton paper) is the standard. It reproduces grain and fine lines well and suits editions of one.
  • Sign and number editions, and keep the seed with your records. A generative print's "negative" is its seed and code.

Printing with separate inks

Screen printing and risograph printing lay down one ink at a time, each from its own plate. They cannot print a full-colour image directly. You supply one black-and-white image per ink, and the colours appear where the inks overlap. This suits generative work very well, because you already build your image in layers: export each layer as its own plate.

Pen plotters

A pen plotter moves a real pen over real paper. It is the machine the first computer artists used in the 1960s, and it has had a revival because the results look hand-made: ink pools, lines vary, paper has tooth. A plotter takes an SVG and draws each path in order.

Plotters impose a particular discipline:

  • Lines only. There are no fills. Tone comes from hatching, stippling or line density (Lesson 10, Maths 5).
  • The pen has a width, typically 0.3 to 0.5 mm. Lines closer together than that merge into a blot. Many overlapping lines in one spot will soak through or tear the paper.
  • Hidden lines must be removed by you. On screen, a filled shape hides what is behind it. A pen cannot do that, so overlaps have to be clipped away in the geometry.
  • One pen at a time. For several colours, export one file or layer per pen and swap pens between them.
  • Time is real. A dense drawing can take hours. Path order matters a great deal.

Ordering the paths

Your code generates paths in whatever order the algorithm produced them, often scattered all over the page. Between paths the plotter lifts the pen and travels, drawing nothing. Sorting the paths so that each begins near where the previous one ended can cut plotting time in half. The simplest good method is greedy: from where the pen is, always go to the nearest unvisited path, drawing it backwards if its far end is closer.

Other steps plotter artists apply before sending a file: joining paths whose ends touch into one continuous line, removing duplicate lines, dropping paths too short to see, and simplifying curves that have more points than the machine can resolve. Free tools exist that do all of this to an SVG, so you only need to produce clean paths.

Laser cutting and engraving

A laser cutter follows vector paths with a beam that burns through sheet material: plywood, acrylic, card, leather, fabric. The same machine can cut (all the way through), score (a shallow line) or engrave (burn away an area). By convention the file tells them apart by colour: for example red hairlines to cut, blue to score, black fills to engrave. Check the convention for the machine you use.

The rules that catch people out:

  • Kerf. The beam removes a strip about 0.1 to 0.3 mm wide. Parts come out slightly small and holes slightly large. For pieces that slot together, offset the paths by half the kerf.
  • Islands fall out. Cut the letter O and its middle drops away. Any enclosed region is a separate piece. Either accept that, or add small bridges that keep it attached, as a stencil does.
  • Minimum web. The material left between two cuts must be wide enough to survive: a rule of thumb is at least the thickness of the sheet for wood, more for brittle acrylic.
  • Closed paths. A shape to be cut out must be one closed loop, not a set of separate segments that happen to meet.
  • Cut order. Inner holes before outer outlines, so the part does not shift before its details are cut.
  • Material safety. Some plastics must never be laser cut. PVC and vinyl release chlorine gas that harms people and destroys the machine. Use only materials the workshop has approved.

A panel with rules built in

Perforated panels are a staple of architecture and product design: screens, speaker grilles, lampshades, ventilation covers. A field decides how large each hole is, so that a pattern appears across the sheet. The engineering lives in the constraints. Below, the largest hole is capped so that the web between neighbours never falls under the minimum you set, whatever the field asks for. The export is an SVG in true millimetres, ready for a laser cutter.

Notice the readout of open area. For a ventilation grille or an acoustic panel that percentage is the specification, and the pattern is free to be anything that meets it. That is the general shape of procedural design in engineering: a number or a rule is required, and the designer has all the remaining freedom.

CNC routing and milling

A CNC router or mill carves material with a spinning cutter. Compared with a laser it adds two things: the cutter is far wider than a beam, and it can cut to any depth, so it can make reliefs and true 3D surfaces.

  • Tool radius. A 6 mm cutter cannot make an inside corner sharper than a 3 mm radius. Either design with rounded inside corners, or add small relief notches ("dog-bones") where a square part must fit.
  • Tool offset. To cut a shape to size, the centre of the cutter must travel half a tool-width outside the line. That offset path is built from normals, exactly as in Maths 3. CAM software normally does this for you.
  • Depth per pass. Material is removed in layers, typically no deeper than half the cutter's diameter each time.
  • Holding the work. A part cut completely free will be thrown by the cutter. Leave small tabs joining it to the sheet and trim them afterwards.
  • Reliefs from height maps. A greyscale image in which brightness means height can be carved directly. Any field from this course, noise terrain, reaction–diffusion, a wave pattern, becomes a carved panel this way.

G-code

Every CNC machine and 3D printer is finally driven by G-code: a plain text list of moves, one per line. Normally a CAM program or slicer writes it for you. It is still worth being able to read, and for simple 2D work you can generate it directly.

CommandMeaning
G21, G90Use millimetres. Coordinates are absolute positions.
G0 X10 Y20Move quickly to (10, 20) without cutting
G1 X30 Y20 F600Move in a straight line to (30, 20), cutting, at 600 mm per minute
G1 Z-0.5 F150Plunge the tool 0.5 mm into the material, slowly
M3 S12000, M5Spindle on at 12,000 rpm, spindle off

Before running G-code you wrote yourself. Machines differ: the commands above are common but not universal, and feeds, speeds and depths depend on the material and cutter. Always load the file in a G-code simulator first, check where the machine's origin is, run it once above the work ("air cutting"), and follow the workshop's safety rules. A wrong number in G-code can break a cutter or injure someone.

3D printing

A 3D printer builds an object in thin horizontal layers. It needs a closed 3D surface, and a program called a slicer turns that into G-code. Getting from a 2D procedural habit to a 3D object is easier than it sounds, because most routes start from techniques you already have.

RouteHowMakes
ExtrudeGive a 2D outline a thicknessCoasters, pendants, stamps, cookie cutters, signs
Height mapRaise each point of a grid by a field's valueTerrain models, textured tiles, lithophanes (reliefs that show an image when lit from behind)
Revolve or stackSweep a radius function round an axis, level by levelVases, lampshades, cups, planters
Implicit surfaceDefine a 3D distance function, as in Lesson 9, and extract its zero surface as trianglesBlobby organic forms, lattices, anything made with smooth unions
Grown geometryRun a 3D simulation and thicken the resultCoral, branching structures, cellular forms

What the printer needs

  • A watertight mesh. The surface must be closed with no holes, with every triangle facing outward. A shape with zero thickness, or one open at an end, cannot be printed.
  • Overhangs. Each layer rests on the one below. Surfaces that lean out more than about 45° from vertical need support material, which leaves scars. Designing within 45° avoids it.
  • Wall thickness. Walls should be at least two nozzle widths: about 0.8 mm for the usual 0.4 mm nozzle. Thinner features disappear.
  • A flat base. The first layer must grip the bed.
  • Size. The object must fit the printer's build volume, commonly around 200 mm in each direction.
  • Layer lines. Detail is finest across the layers (about 0.4 mm) and coarser between them (0.1 to 0.3 mm). Orient the object so the detail that matters lies in the fine direction.

A generative vase

The vase is the classic first generative print. The whole design is one function: the radius at a given height and angle. A silhouette curve gives the overall form, and a ripple that twists with height gives the surface. Slicers have a "vase mode" that prints such a shape as a single continuous spiral with no seams.

The example checks the steepest overhang against the 45° rule and reports it, then builds the triangles and writes them as an STL file. Note the order of the three corners of each triangle. It is anticlockwise when seen from outside, which is how the file says which side is the outside.

Try it: raise the 0.35 in the profile to 0.9 and watch the overhang check fail. Change the nine ribs to five, or replace the sine with noise sampled round a circle (Lesson 3) for an organic surface. Open the STL in any slicer to see it as a solid.

Procedural design in engineering

In engineering the word is usually parametric or generative design, and the aim is performance: lighter, stiffer, cooler, quieter. The methods are the ones in this course.

A formula as a structure

The gyroid is a surface defined by one line of trigonometry:

sin x · cos y + sin y · cos z + sin z · cos x = 0

It divides space into two interlocking channels with no straight line of weakness in any direction, and it supports itself as it prints. It is used as infill inside 3D-printed parts, in lightweight aerospace brackets, in bone implants and in heat exchangers. Material is placed wherever the formula's value is close to zero. The example shows horizontal slices through it, which is exactly what each printed layer looks like. A thicker wall or a tighter period is one changed number.

A fabrication workflow

  1. Choose the process and material first. They decide what is possible. Ask the workshop or print service for its limits: sheet size, minimum line weight, minimum feature, file format.
  2. Write the limits into the code as named constants in millimetres: MIN_WEB, PEN_WIDTH, KERF, MIN_WALL.
  3. Work in real units. Convert to pixels only for the preview.
  4. Preview honestly. Draw lines at the real pen or cutter width. Print a 1:1 section on an office printer and hold it at arm's length.
  5. Make a small test piece covering the finest detail and the tightest tolerance before committing a full sheet or a long print.
  6. Check the file in the next program along: the plotter software, the laser's driver, the slicer, a G-code simulator. Look at its time and material estimates.
  7. Record everything: seed, code version, material, machine settings. Physical work gets reordered.

Key ideas

  • Design in millimetres. Convert to pixels only to preview.
  • Print needs resolution (about 300 dpi), bleed and printable colours. Layered work separates naturally into ink plates.
  • Plotters draw lines only. Order the paths, respect the pen width, and remove hidden lines yourself.
  • Cutting has kerf, islands, minimum webs and closed paths. Milling adds tool radius, depth and tabs.
  • 3D printing needs a watertight mesh, limited overhangs and a minimum wall.
  • Physical limits are constraints. Put them in the rules and every output is manufacturable.
  • Simulate and test small before committing material or running a machine.

Special challenge 3

Make Something You Can Hold

Take a procedural design all the way to a file that a real machine or print service could accept today. If you have access to the machine, make the object. If not, the verified file is the deliverable.

Choose one route

  • Wall print. A poster at a real paper size, rendered at 300 dpi with bleed, or separated into two or three ink plates.
  • Plotter drawing. An SVG of lines only, sized to a real sheet, with paths ordered to reduce pen-up travel and no spacing below the pen width.
  • Laser-cut piece. A panel, coaster, lampshade or stencil as an SVG in millimetres, with cut, score and engrave separated by colour and no islands that fall out unintentionally.
  • 3D print. A vase, planter, pendant or tile as an STL that passes a slicer's checks.

Requirements

  • All dimensions are in millimetres, declared as named constants at the top of the file.
  • At least three manufacturing constraints for your chosen process are enforced by the code, and the sketch reports whether each is met.
  • The design is seeded, and at least five seeds produce valid, manufacturable results.
  • The on-screen preview is honest: real line widths, true proportions, and the overall size shown.
  • The exported file opens correctly in an independent program: an SVG viewer at the right physical size, a slicer, or a G-code simulator.
  • A short note in the file: material, process, estimated time or cost, and anything you would test first.

Hints

  • Start from a piece you made in an earlier challenge. The flow-field poster suits a plotter, the packed silhouette suits a laser cutter, and a noise terrain suits a 3D-printed tile.
  • Where a constraint is hard to guarantee by construction, measure after generating and reject or repair.
  • For SVG, set width and height with mm units and a viewBox in the same numbers, as in example S3.3.

You are done when

  • Another program reports the size you intended, to the millimetre.
  • You could hand the file to a workshop with one sentence of instructions.
